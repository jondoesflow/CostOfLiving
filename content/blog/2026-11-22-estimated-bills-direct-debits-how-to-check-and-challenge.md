Short answer first: **check whether the bill says it is estimated, read your meter, and send the reading in. If your direct debit still looks wrong, ask your supplier to show how they worked it out, including the meter readings they used. If they won't put it right, make a formal complaint, and after 8 weeks you can go to the Energy Ombudsman.**

Most wrong bills come from a guess that was never corrected, and a reading is how you correct it. Everything below comes from Citizens Advice's energy pages for England and the Octopus Energy public API, read on 13 November 2026.

## How to tell if your bill is estimated

If you don't have a smart meter sending readings automatically, your supplier needs readings from you. Citizens Advice puts it plainly: [if you don't send them readings, they'll estimate your usage](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/your-energy-meter/how-to-read-your-energy-meter/).

An estimate can be well off. The example Citizens Advice gives is a supplier estimating from your winter usage because it never got a summer reading. Your bill should say if it is estimated, so look for that wording next to the readings.

A smart meter doesn't make you immune. If it has stopped sending readings, which can happen after you switch supplier, it is no longer working in smart mode and you are back to reading it yourself.

## Check the bill in four steps

1. **Read the meter.** On a standard digital electricity meter, write down the first five numbers from left to right and ignore any in red. Citizens Advice has [a guide to every meter type](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/your-energy-meter/how-to-read-your-energy-meter/), dials included.
2. **Compare it with the reading on the bill.** A small gap is normal, because you have used energy since the bill date. A big gap, or a bill reading higher than the number on your meter today, is your evidence.
3. **Check it is your meter.** Citizens Advice says to [compare the meter number on the bill](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/problems-with-your-energy-bill/energy-bill-too-high/) (the MPAN for electricity, the MPRN for gas) with the one registered to your address. Being billed for a neighbour's meter is rare, but it happens.
4. **Check the prices.** Compare the unit rate and standing charge with your last bill. A price rise is a real reason for a higher bill, and it doesn't mean the bill is wrong.

If the estimate is too high, send the reading and ask for a corrected bill. Take a photo of the meter while you're there. It costs nothing and it settles arguments.

One thing to be ready for: if the supplier has been guessing low, your first real reading will produce a bigger bill. Citizens Advice is clear that you still have to pay it, but you can ask for help if you can't pay it in one go. I covered that in [behind on your energy bills](/blog/behind-on-energy-bills-hardship-funds-free-debt-advice/).

## Is your direct debit about right?

A fixed monthly direct debit is usually based on an estimate of what you'll use over the year, split into 12 equal payments. So you overpay in summer and underpay in winter, and that is how it is meant to work. A healthy account builds credit through the warm months and spends it in the cold ones.

You can sanity-check the amount yourself:

1. Take your yearly kWh for each fuel from your bill or annual statement.
2. Multiply by the unit rate.
3. Add the standing charge times 365.
4. Add the two fuels together and divide by 12.

Here is a worked example. The usage is my assumption for illustration, not your figure. The prices are Flexible Octopus for London, paid by direct debit, from [Octopus's public API](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/VAR-22-11-01/) on 13 November 2026.

| | Usage | Unit rate | Standing charge | Year's cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electricity | 2,700 kWh | 26.60p per kWh | 41.39p a day | £869.14 |
| Gas | 10,000 kWh | 8.15p per kWh | 29.24p a day | £921.35 |
| **Total** | | | | **£1,790.49** |

Divide by 12 and you get about £149 a month. If that household were paying £150, fine. If it were paying £210 with no debt on the account, I'd want to know why.

Your rates will differ by region and tariff, so use the ones printed on your own bill.

## Why direct debits go up, and how to challenge one

Citizens Advice lists the usual reasons a supplier [increases a direct debit](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/problems-with-your-energy-bill/energy-supplier-has-increased-your-direct-debit/): you've used more than they estimated, or they are recovering money you owe from earlier under-billing. Your supplier must tell you before the payment goes up.

If you don't agree with the new amount:

- **Ask for the working.** Your supplier should explain how they reached the figure and give you the meter readings they used.
- **Check those readings** against your bills and your meter. Remember usage is higher in winter, so a rise in November isn't proof of a mistake.
- **Ask them to lower it** to match what you really use, with your own readings to back that up.
- **Ask about variable direct debit.** Some suppliers will take what you used each month instead of a fixed amount. Bills are lower in summer and higher in winter, so it only suits you if a big January payment won't cause trouble.

## If your account is in credit

You can [claim credit back at any time](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/claim-back-credit/claim-back-credit-from-your-energy-supplier/), according to Citizens Advice. Tell your supplier how much you want and give them an up-to-date meter reading.

Two cautions from the same page. Your supplier might refuse if they have a good reason, but they must explain the decision. And you should probably leave money on the account during summer and autumn to cover the higher costs in winter.

So think about the time of year before you ask. A large credit in March, after the heating season, is the one to question. The same credit in October is probably about to be used.

## Bills for energy used more than a year ago

Suppliers can bill you for energy you used in the past, which is called back billing. But Citizens Advice says a supplier [usually can't charge you for energy used more than 12 months ago](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/problems-with-your-energy-bill/you-havent-received-a-gas-or-electricity-bill-in-a-while/) if you haven't had an accurate bill for it, and your direct debit shouldn't be raised to recover it.

There are limits. The protection doesn't cover a bill that was sent in time and not paid, and it may not apply if you behaved unreasonably, such as refusing access to read the meter. If you think you're covered, write to your supplier and say so. Citizens Advice has a template letter on that page.

## If your supplier won't fix it

[Citizens Advice's complaints steps](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/complain-about-an-energy-company/complain-to-your-energy-supplier/) are:

1. Contact the supplier and explain the problem and what you want done.
2. Note the date, the time and who you spoke to. Follow up in writing with your account number.
3. Send your evidence: meter photos, bills, emails.
4. Make a formal complaint through the supplier's complaints procedure.

The supplier should send a decision letter or a letter of deadlock within 8 weeks. If you get a deadlock letter you're unhappy with, or hear nothing in 8 weeks, you can take it to the Energy Ombudsman, which looks at both sides and can make the supplier change its decision.

## What to do today

Read your meter, photograph it and send the reading in. Then do the four-line sum above with the rates on your bill and compare it with your direct debit.

If the sum shows you're paying roughly the right amount but it's still more than you'd like, the tariff is the thing to look at.
