Can your landlord rent you a cold, leaky home? **In England and Wales, a landlord generally can't let or keep letting a home with an Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) rating below E unless they hold a valid, registered exemption.** If your home is rated F or G, that is worth knowing before you spend your own money on heating it.

I'm not a lawyer, so this is what the government guidance says, with the source linked, and where it stops being clear I say so. This covers England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules, which I haven't covered here.

## What the rule says

The government's [landlord guidance on the minimum energy efficiency standard](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/domestic-private-rented-property-minimum-energy-efficiency-standard-landlord-guidance) (checked on 5 November 2026) says that since 1 April 2020, landlords can't let or continue to let covered properties rated below E unless they hold a valid exemption. It applies to most private tenancies that legally need an EPC.

So for a covered tenancy, E, D, C, B or A is allowed. F or G is not, unless there is an exemption on the register. The rules only cover homes let on an assured, regulated or domestic agricultural tenancy that legally needs an EPC. The guidance says a property outside that may be let at F or G, so if you are a lodger or your tenancy is unusual, check with Citizens Advice or your council.

## Check your EPC first

An EPC rates a home from A (most efficient) to G (least). You can look yours up on GOV.UK's [find an energy certificate](https://www.gov.uk/find-energy-certificate) service by postcode, street or certificate number. Your landlord should also be able to give you the certificate. If the lookup says the certificate has expired, or finds none, ask your landlord about it.

The certificate also lists recommended improvements, which is useful for the conversation that follows.

## If it's F or G

The guidance suggests asking your landlord whether the rating is E or higher and, if it's F or G, whether they have registered an exemption. Landlords who rent out an F or G home without a valid exemption are breaking the rules.

Exemptions do exist. According to the same guidance they include:

| Exemption | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| All relevant improvements made | The home still falls short after every suitable measure has been done |
| High cost | The cheapest recommended measure costs more than the £3,500 cap (including VAT), backed by three quotes |
| Wall insulation | A relevant expert says the work would damage the building |
| Third-party consent | Consent was refused or came with conditions that can't reasonably be met |
| Property devaluation | A RICS-registered surveyor says the work would cut the value by more than 5% |
| Recently became a landlord | Lasts six months from the date they took over |

Most last five years. The cost cap means landlords aren't currently required to spend more than £3,500 including VAT on improvements. The guidance says the cap does not apply if third-party funding covers the whole cost of reaching band E, in which case the landlord should use that funding. So it is fair to ask whether a grant could pay for the work. You can search the public Exemptions Register to see whether a property is listed and whether penalties have been issued, via the guidance page above.

If a landlord says they have an exemption, it is reasonable to ask which one.

## Who enforces it

Your local council does. The guidance says councils can ask landlords for information, serve a compliance notice up to 12 months after a suspected breach, and issue a penalty notice up to 18 months after one. Penalties can reach a total of £5,000 per property, and there is a separate maximum for renting a non-compliant property for three months or more.

The guidance is written for landlords and doesn't set out how tenants report a problem. So check your own council's website or contact them directly. Having the property address, the EPC rating and any evidence that you're being let a non-compliant home will help.

## Is a bigger change coming?

Possibly, but it isn't law yet. The same guidance says the government aims for as many privately rented homes as possible to reach EPC band C or equivalent by 2030. It also says it is "exploring policy design options following the 2026 consultation" and will say more in due course. No band C requirement has been set, so don't plan around one.

## What to ask for if your home is E, or exempt

The rule is a floor, not a promise of a warm home. An E-rated home can still be draughty. These are fair, low-cost requests:

- Draught-proofing round doors, letterboxes and windows. My guide to [draught-proofing on a budget](/blog/draught-proofing-on-a-budget-uk/) covers what is cheap enough to ask for or do yourself.
- Loft insulation topped up, a cylinder jacket if the hot water tank has none, and working radiator valves.
- Permission for any bigger job, such as secondary glazing or insulation, in writing before you start.

Things you can do without asking are in the [thermal curtains and window film post](/blog/thermal-curtains-window-film-secondary-glazing-renters/), which is written for renters.

If your landlord does the work, that is the cheapest outcome for you. If they refuse, keep your request in writing. It helps if you later go to the council or ask for advice from a body such as [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/).

## Check what your heating really costs

Whatever the rating says, the bill is what you feel. The [Cheap Watts gas or electric tool](/gas-or-electric/) compares the running cost of gas and electric heating at current rates, so you can see whether a landlord's heating setup is costing you more than it should and have the numbers ready when you ask.

One last point. An EPC is an estimate of how a home performs on paper, not a record of your actual bills. Use it as a conversation starter, then judge the home by how warm it is and what it costs to run.
