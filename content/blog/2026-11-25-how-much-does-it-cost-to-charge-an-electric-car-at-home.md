Short answer: **on 16 November 2026, a typical 30 kWh home charge cost £7.68 on a standard flat tariff in Yorkshire, and between £1.95 and £2.82 on the overnight rate of an electric car tariff. Do that twice a week and the gap is worth about £500 a year.**

That is the headline. The catch is that EV tariffs charge more for everything else you use in the day, so the real answer depends on how much of your electricity the car accounts for. Here is the sum, with the numbers to do it for your own home.

## The simple sum

The cost of a charge is just two numbers multiplied together:

**kWh you put in × the price per kWh = the cost of the charge**

Use the kWh you pay for, which is what the charger's app or your smart meter records, not the size of the battery. A car's own app may show what reached the battery instead, and some energy is lost along the way, so that figure can make a charge look cheaper than it is. Most people top up rather than charge from empty.

Throughout this post I use the same typical figure as the rest of Cheap Watts: **30 kWh on a 7kW home charger, twice a week**. On a 7kW charger that takes about four and a half hours.

How far 30 kWh takes you depends on the car, the weather and how you drive, so I won't guess at a cost per mile. Your car's miles per kWh figure, multiplied by the prices below, gets you there.

## What a charge costs on each tariff

These are the Direct Debit prices for Yorkshire, taken from the Octopus, EDF and E.ON Next price data on 16 November 2026. Flexible Octopus is the standard variable tariff the site uses as its flat comparison.

| Tariff | Cheap window | Cheap rate | Rest of the day | Standing charge | One 30 kWh charge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flexible Octopus (flat) | none | n/a | 25.60p | 60.89p a day | £7.68 |
| Octopus Go | 12.30am to 5.30am | 9.40p | 33.06p | 60.86p a day | £2.82 |
| EDF Go Electric 12m | 11pm to 6am | 6.66p | 33.52p | 66.82p a day | £2.00 |
| E.ON Next Drive Fixed | midnight to 6am | 8.50p | 34.45p | 57.14p a day | £2.55 |
| E.ON Next Drive Smart | midnight to 6am | 6.50p | 33.60p | 57.14p a day | £1.95 |

A few details from the same data that matter before you pick one:

- **Octopus Go** is a variable tariff with no exit fee.
- **EDF Go Electric** is fixed for 12 months, and EDF's own description lists a £75 exit fee.
- **E.ON Next Drive Fixed** is fixed for 12 months, with no exit fee. You need to own or lease an electric or hybrid car.
- **E.ON Next Drive Smart** is also fixed for 12 months, but you need a compatible car and must let E.ON smart charge it through its app. [E.ON says](https://www.eonnext.com/tariffs/next-drive/smart) that if it charges your car during the day, your whole home gets the 6.50p rate while it does.

All of them need a smart meter. Prices vary by region, so your numbers will differ a little from these. The cheap windows above are in UK time as they stood in November; check the supplier's own page for how the window works when the clocks change.

## What it adds up to over a year

Twice a week at 30 kWh is about 3,120 kWh a year.

The savings column includes the difference in standing charge. EDF's is higher, which costs about £22 a year, and E.ON's is lower, which is worth about £14.

If you only charge once a week, halve the charging costs. The savings land roughly between £250 and £310 a year.

## The catch: everything else costs more in the day

Every one of these tariffs charges more than the flat rate outside its cheap window. On these prices that premium was:

- Octopus Go: 7.45p more per kWh
- EDF Go Electric: 7.92p more per kWh
- E.ON Next Drive Fixed: 8.85p more per kWh
- E.ON Next Drive Smart: 8.00p more per kWh, except while E.ON is smart charging your car in the day, when the whole home pays 6.50p

So the question is whether the car saves more than the rest of the house costs extra. Here is where each tariff breaks even against the flat rate, assuming none of your home's own use falls in the cheap window. For Drive Smart that is a worst case, because any daytime smart charging also makes the rest of the house cheaper while it runs:

| Tariff | Charging twice a week: break-even daytime use | Charging once a week: break-even daytime use |
|---|---|---|
| Octopus Go | 6,784 kWh a year | 3,393 kWh a year |
| EDF Go Electric 12m | 7,188 kWh a year | 3,457 kWh a year |
| E.ON Next Drive Fixed | 6,185 kWh a year | 3,170 kWh a year |
| E.ON Next Drive Smart | 7,622 kWh a year | 3,896 kWh a year |

If your home uses less than that outside the cheap hours, the EV tariff wins. For a typical two-charges-a-week driver, it would take a very big daytime habit to lose. For a once-a-week driver in a home that uses a lot of electricity in the day, it gets closer, and is worth pricing properly.

That break-even is cautious. Your fridge, freezer and anything on standby also run overnight, and they get the cheap rate too, which tips things further towards the EV tariff.

## Charging from a three-pin plug

Plenty of people start with the cable that came with the car, plugged into an ordinary socket. It works, but there are two things to know.

**It is slower.** The [RAC says](https://www.rac.co.uk/drive/electric-cars/charging/electric-car-charging-how-it-works-and-how-much-it-costs/) most car makers limit the current drawn from a three-pin socket to 10A or less, a maximum of 2.3kW. At that rate, a full five-hour Octopus Go window fits in only about 11.5 kWh, so most of a 30 kWh charge spills into the daytime rate.

**Safety matters more than the saving.** [Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/media-centre/press-releases/2019/05/driven-to-danger-electric-vehicle-drivers-charging-dangerously-due-to-lack-of-public-infrastructure/) advises never using a domestic multi-socket extension lead to charge an electric car, only ever using an extension lead suitable for outdoor use such as a reel cable if you must use one, and never plugging one extension lead into another. It says the safest way to charge at home is a dedicated wallbox charge point, installed by a qualified, registered electrician. If you use a cable reel, its general advice is to unwind it fully so it doesn't overheat.

## How to get the cheapest charge

1. **Check your current rate.** Your bill or supplier app shows your price per kWh. Multiply it by the kWh you put in each week and you have today's cost.
2. **Use the car's timer, or the charger's.** On any tariff with a cheap window, the saving only happens if the car charges inside it. Set the schedule in the car or charger app so it starts after the window opens.
3. **If you're on a flat tariff, look at the timing anyway.** On a flat rate the price is the same all day, so timing won't change your bill. It can still help the grid: I covered when electricity is cleanest and cheapest in [What's the cheapest time to use electricity in the UK?](/blog/cheapest-time-to-use-electricity-uk/)
4. **Price an EV tariff on your own usage.** The break-even table above is a first check. Add up what your home uses outside the hours you'd charge, using your supplier app or smart meter data, and compare it with the figure for the tariff you're looking at.
5. **Read the terms.** Exit fees, fixed terms and car or app requirements differ, as the list above shows.

To see what charging costs at different times of day on today's prices, the [Cheap Watts home page](/) lets you pick the electric car from the list of appliances and shows the cheapest slot to run it.

If you charge twice a week on a standard tariff, the next step is simple: take your own weekly kWh, multiply by the gap between your rate and an EV tariff's night rate, and you will know within a minute whether switching is worth the effort.
