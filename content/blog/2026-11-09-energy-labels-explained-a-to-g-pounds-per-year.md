The letter on an energy label is not the number to buy on. The kWh figure printed on the label is, because you can turn it into pounds. On 2 November 2026 the Flexible Octopus electricity rate in London was 26.6p per kWh, from the [Octopus Energy API](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/VAR-22-11-01/), so every 100kWh on a label costs you about £26.60.

That is the whole trick. Here is how to read each part of the label, the sum for washing machines, dishwashers and fridges, and where the label can mislead you.

## What is on the label

Since 1 March 2021, products such as washing machines, dishwashers, fridges and televisions have carried a [rescaled energy label across the UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-energy-labelling-of-products), on a scale of A to G. Shops and websites have to display it when they sell you the product.

On a washing machine, the [regulation that sets the label](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/2014/data.htm) lists, among other things:

- a QR code that links to the product's details
- the energy class, A to G
- weighted energy consumption per 100 cycles, in kWh
- the rated capacity in kg for the eco 40-60 programme
- the water used per cycle, in litres

Dishwashers get the [same treatment](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/2017/data.htm): the class, plus the eco programme's energy use in kWh per 100 cycles. For fridges and freezers, the [regulation](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/2016/data.htm) defines annual energy consumption in kWh per year.

So the number you want is already there. It just isn't the biggest thing on the label.

## The sum: from kWh to pounds

For anything with "per 100 cycles":

1. Divide the label figure by 100 to get kWh per cycle.
2. Multiply by how many cycles you run in a year.
3. Multiply by your unit rate in pounds per kWh. At 26.6p that is £0.266.

For a fridge or freezer, the label figure is already a year. Multiply it by your unit rate and you are done.

Your own unit rate is on your bill or in your supplier's app, and it will differ from the London figure I used here.

## Worked examples

These labels are made-up examples to show the sum, not real models. Check the label on the one you are looking at.

**Washing machine.** Cheap Watts assumes four washes a week, which is 208 a year.

| Label says | kWh a year | Cost a year at 26.6p |
|---|---|---|
| 50kWh per 100 cycles | 104kWh | about £27.66 |
| 100kWh per 100 cycles | 208kWh | about £55.32 |

The gap is about £27.66 a year. Over a ten-year life that is real money, but not enough to justify a much dearer machine on its own.

**Dishwasher.** Cheap Watts assumes five runs a week, which is 260 a year.

| Label says | kWh a year | Cost a year at 26.6p |
|---|---|---|
| 80kWh per 100 cycles | 208kWh | about £55.32 |
| 100kWh per 100 cycles | 260kWh | about £69.15 |

**Fridge or freezer.** Always on, so the year is the label.

| Label says | Cost a year at 26.6p |
|---|---|
| 100kWh a year | about £26.60 |
| 200kWh a year | about £53.19 |
| 300kWh a year | about £79.79 |

The fridge is where the label matters most. It runs every hour of every day, so a gap of 100kWh a year on the label is £26.60 a year, every year, with no change in your habits.

## Where the label can mislead you

**The letter only compares like with like.** An A for a washing machine and an A for a fridge say nothing about each other. Even within one type, two machines in the same class can use quite different amounts, because a class covers a band of values. Use the kWh figure to compare.

**It is a test figure, not your figure.** The washing machine number is for the eco 40-60 programme, and the dishwasher number is for the eco programme. If you always run a quick wash or a hot cycle, you will use more. The label tells you how machines compare under the same test, which is still useful.

**Capacity matters.** A 10kg drum and a 7kg drum with the same kWh per 100 cycles are not equally efficient per kilo of washing. If you wash small loads, a bigger drum buys you little.

**Old labels use other scales.** If you are looking at a second-hand or older appliance, its label may not use the current scale, so do not compare a letter across the two. The kWh figure still works, though check what it measures.

**Savings depend on use.** A dishwasher you run twice a week saves less from a better label than one you run every day. The sum above takes five seconds, so do it with your own number of cycles.

## What I would do before buying

1. Find the kWh figure on the label (or the QR code page) for each model you are considering.
2. Work out the yearly cost with your own number of uses and your own unit rate.
3. Take the difference between the two models and multiply by the years you expect to keep it.
4. Set that against the price gap. If a dearer model saves £10 a year and costs £150 more, it will not pay back in any sensible time.

## The short version

- The kWh figure on the label is the number to compare, not the letter.
- Washing machines and dishwashers show kWh per 100 cycles. Fridges show kWh per year.
- At 26.6p per kWh (London, Flexible Octopus, 2 November 2026), 100kWh costs about £26.60.
- The label is a test figure under a set programme, so your own use will differ.

This week, find the label (or model number) on your fridge or freezer, as it runs all year, and multiply its yearly kWh by your unit rate. That is the quickest sum in your kitchen.
