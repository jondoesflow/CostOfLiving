Is an oil-filled radiator cheaper to run than a fan heater? **At the same wattage and the same setting, they cost exactly the same: every unit of electricity either one uses ends up as heat in your room. A 2kW heater costs about 53p an hour at full power. The difference is in how they deliver that heat, which changes how long you need them on.**

So the honest answer is that neither is cheap. Below is the sum, where the two genuinely differ, and what to use instead.

## The prices I used

These are the Flexible Octopus standard rates for London (region C), including VAT, from the Octopus API on 6 November 2026:

| Fuel | Unit rate |
|---|---|
| Electricity | 26.6p per kWh |
| Gas | 8.15p per kWh |

Your region and supplier will differ a little. The [Cheap Watts](/) calculator uses live prices if you want your own figure.

## Why the wattage is the whole story

An electric heater turns its electricity into heat. A 2kW heater uses 2 kWh in an hour, whatever shape it comes in:

- 2 kWh x 26.6p = **about 53p an hour at full power**
- Three hours of that is about £1.60

The wattage is printed on the heater. A 1kW model costs half as much per hour and puts out half the heat, so it does not save you anything per unit of warmth. That is the trap with "low energy" heaters: a lower number on the label means less heat, not cheaper heat.

## Where an oil-filled radiator and a fan heater do differ

[Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/products-and-appliances/heating-cooling/portable-heaters/) describes the difference like this. A fan heater heats the air and blows it into the room, so it is quick but the warmth stops when you switch it off. An oil-filled radiator heats a reservoir of oil, takes longer to warm up, and keeps giving out heat after it is switched off. They say oil-filled radiators are generally more economical to run.

The way I read that: the saving is not in the watts. It comes from how you use them.

- **A fan heater suits a short burst.** If you are warming a cold bathroom for ten minutes, speed matters and the stored heat of an oil radiator is wasted.
- **An oil-filled radiator suits a room you sit in for hours.** With a working thermostat it can switch itself off once the room is warm and coast on the heat stored in the oil.

I have no verified figure for how much the coasting saves, so I will not put a percentage on it. Here is what it could look like, using assumptions I have made up purely to show the arithmetic. If a thermostat meant a 2kW heater only drew power for half of a three-hour evening, that is 3 kWh, about 80p instead of £1.60. Your result depends on your room, your thermostat setting and how well the room holds heat, so treat that as an illustration, not a promise.

## What a winter of heater use adds up to

The typical figure the site uses for an electric heater is 2kW for two hours, five times a week:

- 4 kWh a time x 5 = 20 kWh a week
- 20 kWh x 26.6p = **about £5.32 a week**
- Over a 26 week heating season (my assumption), about £138

That is one room for two hours at full power. Leave one running through a working day and the number climbs quickly.

## Heating one room with gas is cheaper per unit

If you have gas central heating, the same heat costs much less. I have assumed a boiler turns 85% of its gas into heat. That is my working figure, not a measurement of your boiler.

- Gas: 8.15p divided by 0.85 = about 9.6p per kWh of heat
- Electric heater: 26.6p per kWh of heat

So 2 kWh of heat costs about 19p from the boiler against 53p from a plug-in heater, roughly 2.8 times as much. A heater earns its place when you only want one small room warm and the rest of the house cold, but if you are warming several rooms the gas system usually wins. ## Cheaper ways to feel warm

1. **Heat the person, not the room.** An electric blanket uses about 100W, so about 2.7p an hour on the prices above, around 5% of a 2kW heater. I worked this through in my post on [what an electric blanket costs to run](/blog/electric-blanket-running-cost-uk/).
2. **Stop the heat escaping.** Thick curtains and draught strips help any heater work less. My posts on [thermal curtains and window film](/blog/thermal-curtains-window-film-secondary-glazing-renters/) and [draught-proofing](/blog/draught-proofing-on-a-budget-uk/) cover the cheap options.
3. **Use a cheap-rate window if you are on a time-of-use tariff.** Running a heater at the cheapest time of day changes the maths. The [Cheap Watts tool](/) shows when that is.
4. **Use a thermostat, and a thermometer.** A heater with a thermostat stops once the room is warm. A cheap room thermometer tells you whether it is.

## Using a portable heater safely

Electrical Safety First's key points for portable heaters:

- Use a UK 3-pin plug, and avoid buying second-hand.
- Buy from a reputable retailer, not a third-party online seller.
- Keep it at least one metre from combustible things like furniture and curtains, on a level surface where it cannot be knocked over.
- Never dry clothes on it, and do not use one overnight.
- Keep smoke alarms or heat detectors nearby.

Their [portable heaters guidance](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/products-and-appliances/heating-cooling/portable-heaters/) has the full list.

## What to do next

Look at the wattage on your heater and multiply it by 26.6p (or your own rate) to get the cost of an hour at full power. If it is over 50p, ask whether a blanket, a door brush or a closed curtain would do the job instead. A smart plug with energy monitoring will show you what yours really uses over a week.
