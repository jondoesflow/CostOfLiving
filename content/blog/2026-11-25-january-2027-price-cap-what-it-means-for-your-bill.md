Short answer first: **from 1 January 2027 the price cap is £2,166 a year for a typical household paying by Direct Debit, up 26% from £1,723. Electricity goes from 26.32p to 32.69p per kWh, and gas from 7.97p to 10.96p per kWh. Standing charges barely move.**

So almost all of the rise is in the price of each unit. That matters, because it means the more you use, the harder January hits.

Every new figure here is from Ofgem's announcement on 25 November 2026: its [price cap unit rates page](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/your-energy-supply/your-energy-bill/energy-price-cap-unit-rates-and-standing-charges) and its [summary letter for 1 January to 31 March 2027](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2026-11/summary-of-changes-to-energy-price-cap-1-January-to-31-March-2027.pdf). I read both this morning.

## The new rates, side by side

These are Ofgem's Direct Debit figures averaged across England, Scotland and Wales:

| | 1 October to 31 December 2026 | 1 January to 31 March 2027 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electricity unit rate | 26.32p per kWh | 32.69p per kWh |
| Electricity standing charge | 54.83p a day | 54.77p a day |
| Gas unit rate | 7.97p per kWh | 10.96p per kWh |
| Gas standing charge | 29.68p a day | 29.65p a day |

That's 6.37p more for every kWh of electricity (up about 24%) and 2.99p more for every kWh of gas (up about 38%). The standing charges actually drop by a fraction of a penny.

Two notes from Ofgem. Gas includes 5% VAT. Electricity has no VAT from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027, so it's shown without any.

Your own rates depend on your region and how you pay. Ofgem's page has a table for every region, and for prepayment and standard credit too. On its typical figures, the yearly cap is £2,108 for prepayment, £2,328 for standard credit and £1,265 for Economy 7 on Direct Debit.

## Why it's gone up

Ofgem's summary letter puts it down to wholesale costs. The wholesale part of the cap is up 50% and now makes up 56% of it. Ofgem points to the conflict and instability in the Middle East, and to historically low European gas storage going into winter.

Gas is the bigger driver. Ofgem says the gas wholesale allowance is up 59% and the electricity one is up 39%, and that electricity follows gas because gas still sets the price of power much of the time.

## What it means for your own bill

The £2,166 headline uses Ofgem's typical household: 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year. Yours will be different.

In my [price cap explainer](/blog/ofgem-price-cap-explained-how-it-affects-your-bill/) I worked through a lower and a higher user. Here are the same two households at the new rates. The usage figures are my illustrations, not Ofgem's, so swap in your own.

| | Lower use household | Higher use household |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electricity used | 1,500 kWh a year | 2,500 kWh a year |
| Gas used | 5,000 kWh a year | 10,000 kWh a year |
| Electricity units | £490.35 | £817.25 |
| Electricity standing charge (365 days) | £199.91 | £199.91 |
| Gas units | £548.00 | £1,096.00 |
| Gas standing charge (365 days) | £108.22 | £108.22 |
| **Total at January rates** | **£1,346.48** | **£2,221.38** |
| Total at October rates | £1,101.76 | £1,763.46 |
| **Difference** | **£244.72** | **£457.92** |

Those are yearly figures, as if the January rates held for twelve months. They won't, because Ofgem sets the cap every three months. But they show the shape: the higher user's bill goes up by nearly twice as much, because the rise is in the unit rates, not the standing charges.

For a single appliance it's simpler. Anything that uses 1 kWh now costs 32.69p instead of 26.32p. Our [running cost pages](/cost-to-run/) show what each appliance uses, so you can see where your electricity actually goes.

## If you're on a fixed deal

Nothing changes until your deal ends. Ofgem is clear that the cap doesn't protect you if you've agreed a fixed tariff, and that cuts both ways: your unit rates stay as agreed.

Check the end date, though. If your fix finishes in January or February, you'll roll onto your supplier's default tariff, which means these new capped rates.

## Two things from the Budget that also land in January

I covered these in my [Autumn Budget post](/blog/autumn-budget-2026-energy-bills-what-to-expect/). Both are confirmed in the [Budget 2026 document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html):

- **A £100 Household Energy Payment from January 2027,** for households in England in council tax bands A to D, delivered through the council tax system. You shouldn't need to apply. Ignore anyone who texts or calls asking for bank details to "claim" it.
- **No VAT on household electricity until July 2027.** That's already in the rates above. The Budget expects the extension to take around £50 off the annual cap in April 2027.

To put the £100 in context: on Ofgem's typical figures the cap is £443 a year higher from January. The payment helps, but it doesn't cover the rise.

## When does it start, and what's next?

The new cap applies from 1 January 2027 to 31 March 2027. Ofgem sets it every three months, so the next change is from 1 April 2027.

## Three things to do this week

1. **Check whether you're on a fixed deal, and when it ends.** It's on your supplier's app or your latest bill. If you're on a default tariff, it's worth comparing a fixed deal against these new rates before January, using your own usage rather than the national average. Our [Should I switch? tool](/should-i-switch/) works it out from your smart meter data, including time-of-use tariffs.
2. **Read your meter just before 1 January.** Take a photo and send the reading to your supplier. That way the energy you used in December is billed at December's rates, not estimated across the change.
3. **See what your appliances cost at the new rate.** Use Cheap Watts to look up the washing machine, tumble dryer, oven or electric shower. If you're on a time-of-use tariff, it'll also show the cheapest time to run them.

## The short version

- From 1 January 2027 the cap is £2,166 a year for a typical Direct Debit household, up 26%.
- Electricity is 32.69p per kWh and gas 10.96p per kWh. Standing charges are almost unchanged.
- Fixed deals aren't affected until they end.
- The £100 payment for bands A to D in England arrives from January, but it doesn't cover the rise.
