Short answer: **a typical 250W dehumidifier uses a quarter of a unit of electricity an hour. On a standard flat tariff in Yorkshire on 21 November 2026, that was about 6.4p an hour, or 51p for an eight hour session. Four sessions a week comes to about £106.51 a year.**

That's cheaper than most people expect, and a lot cheaper than a tumble dryer or an electric heater. But it isn't nothing, and it adds up quickly if you leave one running all day, every day.

Here's the sum, what it costs on different tariffs and in different parts of the country, and how to bring it down.

## The sum

Cheap Watts uses the same typical figure for a dehumidifier everywhere on the site: **250W for eight hours, which is 2 kWh, four times a week.**

To work out your own:

**watts ÷ 1,000 × hours = kWh, then kWh × your price per kWh = the cost**

At the Flexible Octopus Direct Debit rate for Yorkshire on 21 November 2026, 25.60p per kWh:

- One hour at 250W is 0.25 kWh, so about **6.4p**.
- An eight hour session is 2 kWh, so about **51.2p**.
- Four sessions a week is 416 kWh a year, so about **£106.51**.

Your own machine might use more or less. The wattage is on the label on the back or in the manual. Many models also have a humidity setting, and if yours does, check the manual for how it behaves once the room reaches that level, because it may not draw full power all the time. The only way to know for sure is to measure it, which I'll come to.

## What it costs where you live

Electricity prices differ by region. These are the Flexible Octopus Direct Debit unit rates from the [Octopus price data](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/) on 21 November 2026, for the cheapest and dearest regions and Yorkshire, using the typical 2 kWh session.

| Region | Price per kWh | Per hour (250W) | Eight hour session | Four a week for a year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| East Midlands (cheapest) | 25.36p | 6.3p | 50.7p | £105.48 |
| Yorkshire | 25.60p | 6.4p | 51.2p | £106.51 |
| Merseyside and North Wales (dearest) | 27.86p | 7.0p | 55.7p | £115.91 |

So across the country, the same habit costs somewhere between about £105 and £116 a year on that tariff. The difference between regions is far smaller than the difference between running it four days a week and running it every day: seven sessions a week in Yorkshire would be about £186 a year.

## Does the time of day matter?

Only if your tariff changes price through the day. On a flat tariff, an hour at 3am costs the same as an hour at 6pm.

On a time-of-use tariff, it can matter a lot. Here are the Yorkshire rates for two Octopus tariffs on 21 November 2026, for an eight hour session:

| Tariff and timing | What you pay | Eight hour session |
|---|---|---|
| Octopus Go, all eight hours outside the cheap window | 33.06p | 66.1p |
| Octopus Go, 12.30am to 8.30am (five cheap hours at 9.40p, three at 33.06p) | mixed | 36.5p |
| Cosy Octopus, all eight hours at the standard rate | 27.72p | 55.4p |
| Cosy Octopus, three hours from 4pm to 7pm only | 41.58p | 31.2p for those three hours |
| Cosy Octopus, three hours from 1pm to 4pm only | 13.59p | 10.2p for those three hours |

On Cosy that day, the cheap hours were 4am to 7am, 1pm to 4pm and 10pm to midnight, and 4pm to 7pm was the dearest. Octopus describes Go as "the smart EV tariff" and Cosy as "a heat pump tariff", so check whether you qualify before planning around either. I covered whether these tariffs make sense for everyone else in [Is a time-of-use tariff worth it if you don't have an electric car?](/blog/time-of-use-tariff-worth-it-without-electric-car/)

The catch with the cheapest hours is that they tend to be the middle of the night. [Devon and Somerset Fire and Rescue](https://www.dsfire.gov.uk/news/safety/why-you-shouldnt-use-washing-machines-and-tumble-dryers-night) says: "The safest time to use electricals is when you are awake, and definitely in the house." So on Cosy, the 1pm to 4pm window is the one worth using if someone's home in the afternoon. On Go, the cheap hours finish at 5.30am, which only really helps early risers.

## How it compares with other ways to dry washing

A lot of people buy a dehumidifier to dry clothes indoors in winter. Here's how that stacks up against the alternatives, using the site's typical figures and the same Yorkshire flat rate of 25.60p on 21 November 2026.

| Method | Typical use | Cost each time |
|---|---|---|
| Dehumidifier, 250W for eight hours | 2 kWh | 51.2p |
| Heated airer, about 300W for four hours | 1.2 kWh | 30.7p |
| Heat pump tumble dryer, full load | 1.5 kWh | 38.4p |
| Condenser or vented tumble dryer, full load | 4 kWh | £1.02 |

The heated airer looks cheapest, but it puts all the water from your washing into the room. The dehumidifier is the one that takes it out again. In practice many people use both together, or run the dehumidifier in the room where the washing is drying with the door closed. I went through the airer and dryer numbers in more detail in [Heated airer vs tumble dryer](/blog/heated-airer-vs-tumble-dryer-running-cost/).

## Why it can be worth the money

Drying washing indoors adds moisture to the air. The UK government's [guidance on damp and mould](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/damp-and-mould-understanding-and-addressing-the-health-risks-for-rented-housing-providers/understanding-and-addressing-the-health-risks-of-damp-and-mould-in-the-home--2) lists "showering, drying laundry and cooking" among the everyday sources of moisture that build up in a home if it can't get out.

That matters for more than the paintwork. The same guidance says: "Damp and mould primarily affect the airways and lungs, but they can also affect the eyes and skin." It also says that "people with certain health conditions, children and older adults are at greater risk of more severe health impacts."

The guidance's own suggestions for reducing moisture are mostly free: extractor fans in kitchens and bathrooms, closing internal doors when cooking or showering, covering pans, drying laundry outdoors if possible and opening windows now and then. A dehumidifier doesn't replace any of that. But if your washing has to dry inside for half the year, 51p a session is a fair price for keeping the water out of your walls.

If you rent and the damp is coming from the building rather than your washing, that's a conversation for your landlord, not a job for a dehumidifier.

## How to cut the cost

1. **Close the door.** A dehumidifier in a small, closed room with the washing does its job much faster than one trying to dry the whole house.
2. **Use the humidity setting if it has one.** Running flat out all day when the room is already dry is paying for nothing.
3. **Don't run it for longer than you need.** Eight hours is the site's typical figure, not a rule. If the washing's dry in five, that's 19p saved in Yorkshire at 21 November prices.
4. **Use the free fixes first.** Open a window for a bit, use the extractor fan, put a lid on the pan. Less moisture in the air means less work for the dehumidifier.
5. **Measure yours.** An energy monitoring plug shows exactly what your machine uses in a session. Swap that figure into the sum above instead of the typical 2 kWh.
