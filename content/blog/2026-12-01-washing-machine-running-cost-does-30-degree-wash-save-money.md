Short answer: **yes, it does. Which? testing found that washing at 30°C rather than 40°C uses 38% less energy. On a typical 40°C wash costing about 20p, that's about 8p back every load, or about £16 a year at four washes a week.**

That's on a standard flat tariff in Yorkshire on 22 November 2026. It isn't life changing money, but it's one of the few savings that costs nothing, takes no effort and works on every tariff.

Here's the sum, where the money goes inside the machine, and when a hotter wash is still worth it.

## What one wash costs

Cheap Watts uses the same typical figure for a washing machine everywhere on the site: **0.8 kWh for a 40°C cotton wash, which takes about two hours, four times a week.**

The cost of a wash is:

**kWh per wash × your price per kWh = the cost of the wash**

At the Flexible Octopus Direct Debit rate for Yorkshire on 22 November 2026, 25.60p per kWh, a 40°C wash costs about 20.5p. Four a week is 208 washes a year, which comes to **£42.60 a year**.

Your own machine will differ. The energy label shows energy use in kWh per 100 cycles, measured on the eco 40-60 programme. I went through how to turn any label into pounds in [Energy labels explained](/blog/energy-labels-explained-a-to-g-pounds-per-year/).

## Why temperature matters so much

Most of the electricity a washing machine uses goes on heating the water at the start of the cycle, and that's how Cheap Watts counts it. Turning the drum and pumping water out use far less.

So the temperature dial is really a dial for how much water you pay to heat. Going from 40°C to 30°C means heating the same water 10 degrees less, and the energy use drops a lot further than you might expect.

[Which? tested this](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/change-this-laundry-habit-and-help-cut-co2-pollution-by-400000-cars-aVQXA7B7HxFx) (April 2020) and found:

- washing at 30°C rather than 40°C used **38% less energy**
- going down to 20°C cut energy use by **62%**

Those are averages from their testing, and every machine is a bit different, but the direction is clear.

## What 30°C and 20°C are worth in pounds

Here's a worked example. It takes the site's typical 0.8 kWh at 40°C, applies the Which? percentages, and prices it at the Flexible Octopus Yorkshire rate of 25.60p per kWh on 22 November 2026, at four washes a week.

| Wash temperature | Energy per wash | Cost per wash | Cost a year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40°C | 0.80 kWh | 20.5p | £42.60 |
| 30°C (38% less) | 0.50 kWh | 12.7p | £26.41 |
| 20°C (62% less) | 0.30 kWh | 7.8p | £16.19 |

So moving every wash from 40°C to 30°C saves about 63 kWh a year, which is **about £16.19**. Going all the way to 20°C saves about 103 kWh, or **about £26.41**.

If you wash more than four times a week, or your machine runs a 60°C programme as a habit, the saving is bigger. If you already wash at 30°C, you've already banked it.

## Does it matter which tariff you're on?

Turning the temperature down saves money on every tariff, because you use fewer kWh whatever the price. Timing is a separate saving, and it only helps if your price changes through the day.

These are the Octopus Go unit rates for Yorkshire from the [Octopus price data](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/) on 22 November 2026:

| Tariff and time | Price per kWh | 40°C wash | 30°C wash |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexible Octopus (flat), any time | 25.60p | 20.5p | 12.7p |
| Octopus Go, 12.30am to 5.30am | 9.40p | 7.5p | 4.7p |
| Octopus Go, rest of the day | 33.06p | 26.4p | 16.4p |

Two things to bear in mind. Octopus describes Go as "the smart EV tariff", so check you qualify before you plan around it. And a time-of-use tariff charges more outside its cheap hours, so price your whole day, not one appliance.

On a flat tariff, 30°C is the saving. Timing makes no difference to the price at all.

## The safety point: not while you sleep

The cheapest hours on most time-of-use tariffs are the middle of the night, and that's when fire services would rather your washing machine was off.

[Devon and Somerset Fire and Rescue](https://www.dsfire.gov.uk/news/safety/why-you-shouldnt-use-washing-machines-and-tumble-dryers-night) says: "The safest time to use electricals is when you are awake, and definitely in the house." It also says "You should never leave appliances on when you're out."

On Octopus Go, if you're up early, starting a wash before 5.30am means the heating at the start of the cycle gets the cheap rate while you're awake for the rest. Otherwise, a cool wash in the daytime is the safe, cheap option.

## When a hotter wash is still worth it

Which? says modern machines still do a good job at lower temperatures most of the time. It flags two exceptions:

- tough protein stains, such as blood, won't come out as well at 30°C
- synthetic fibres need more heat for a thorough clean

So 30°C as your everyday setting, with a hotter wash when the load needs it, is a sensible default. Always check the care label on anything you're not sure about.

## Other ways to cut the cost

1. **Fill the drum.** Fewer, fuller loads mean fewer litres of water to heat. Going from four washes a week to three saves 52 washes a year, about £10.65 at 40°C on the flat rate above.
2. **Use the eco 40-60 programme where it suits.** It's the programme the energy label is measured on, so its figure is the one you can compare. It often runs for hours: that's normal, as it heats less and makes up for it with time.
3. **Think about the drying.** Washing is cheap next to tumble drying. Cheap Watts uses 4.0 kWh for a tumble dryer load, five times the 0.8 kWh of the wash itself. I compared the options in [Heated airer vs tumble dryer](/blog/heated-airer-vs-tumble-dryer-running-cost/).
4. **Measure your own machine.** An energy monitoring plug shows what a wash really uses at each temperature, so you can swap your own numbers into the sum above.
