If you rent and can't change the windows, **the best value is usually well-fitting thermal curtains drawn at dusk, then window insulation film for the draughtiest or single-glazed windows. Secondary glazing does more, but costs more and needs your landlord's say-so.**

None of these will transform your bill on its own. Windows are one leak among several, so I'll show you a worked sum at today's gas price to keep expectations honest, and then how to choose.

## Why windows matter

A government buyer's guide to glazing, quoting Energy Saving Trust figures, says [18% of heat loss occurs through windows](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a802d7aed915d74e33f8e80/DECC%5Ffactsheet%5F11.11.16%5FGLAZING%5FLOCKED.pdf). It also says heat is lost through single glazing around twice as fast as through standard double glazing.

That guide is from 2016 and the 18% is a typical figure for a home, not your home. Treat it as a reasonable starting point rather than a promise.

## Option 1: thermal curtains

The same guide says well-fitting curtains and blinds can reduce heat loss through a window if drawn at dusk. It also suggests keeping them open on sunny days when the sun shines in, to make the most of free warmth.

What I'd look for:

- **Lined or thermal-backed curtains.** Thicker cloth gives you more of a barrier than thin cotton.
- **A close fit.** Curtains that hang to the sill or floor and sit wide of the window frame do more than short ones with a gap.
- **Hanging them on a tension rod or existing pole.** No drilling, so your deposit is safe, and you can take them with you when you move.

I haven't quoted a price or a saving per curtain, because I don't have a reliable source for either. Check the price on the shelf, and the label for the thermal rating where one is given.

## Option 2: window insulation film

Film is a clear plastic sheet that you fix over the window frame, usually with double-sided tape, then shrink tight with a hairdryer. It makes a still pocket of air between the film and the glass, a bit like a very cheap secondary pane.

It is cheap and removable, which suits renters. The trade-offs are practical:

- You usually can't open that window while the film is on.
- Tape can lift paint or finish from an old frame when you remove it, so test a small hidden patch first.
- It looks less tidy than curtains, and some films reduce the light slightly.

I'd use it on a single-glazed or very draughty window you don't open in winter, such as a bedroom or stairwell window, and leave the rest alone.

## Option 3: secondary glazing

Secondary glazing is a second pane and frame fitted inside the existing window. The same government guide says it won't be as well sealed as a double-glazing unit, but is much cheaper to fit and will still save energy.

For renters, the sticking point is permission. It is usually a fixed fitting, so ask your landlord first. The guide also notes that if a building is listed, it is unlikely you will get permission to change the windows, and secondary glazing and good blinds or curtains may be the best option. Your local planning office can tell you where you stand.

If your landlord is open to it, that's the point to ask them to pay or share the cost, since the improvement stays with the property.

## What they might save: a worked example

I can't give you a trustworthy per-product saving, so here is a sum you can redo with your own numbers. The assumptions are mine.

On 1 November 2026, the Flexible Octopus gas rate in London was about 8.15p per kWh including VAT (from the [Octopus Energy API](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/VAR-22-11-01/)). Your region will be close and your bill has your exact rate.

Say your home uses 10,000kWh of gas a year for heating. If windows account for 18% of the heat loss, that is about 1,800kWh, or around £147 a year at 8.15p. Now suppose a measure cuts the heat lost through the windows by a fraction. Those fractions are my guesses, not measured results:

| Cut in window heat loss | Gas saved | Saving a year at 8.15p |
|---|---|---|
| 10% (light curtains drawn at dusk) | 180kWh | about £15 |
| 25% (good thermal curtains or film) | 450kWh | about £37 |
| 50% (well-fitted secondary glazing) | 900kWh | about £73 |

This is deliberately simple. It ignores boiler efficiency and how much of your heating the windows really account for. A single-glazed flat could do better than the table, and a home with newer double glazing will do worse.

The honest read: curtains you already own, drawn at dusk, cost nothing and are the first thing to try. Spending on the rest should be judged against savings of tens of pounds a year, plus comfort. A window you can sit next to without a chill is worth something even when the pounds are modest.

## A sensible order for renters

1. **Draw the curtains you have at dusk**, and open them on sunny days.
2. **Fix the draughts first.** Sealing gaps often beats insulating the glass. My [draught-proofing guide](/blog/draught-proofing-on-a-budget-uk/) covers the cheap jobs and the ventilation you must keep.
3. **Add thermal curtains** to the coldest rooms, hung on a tension rod or existing pole.
4. **Use film** on windows you don't open and won't miss.
5. **Ask your landlord about secondary glazing** if the windows are single-glazed or failing.

Don't block trickle vents or air bricks while you do this. The ventilation is there for a reason, and my draught-proofing post links to the NHS carbon monoxide advice.
