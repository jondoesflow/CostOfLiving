Of the three, **bleeding is worth doing if a radiator has cold patches, TRVs are worth using in rooms you don't need warm, and reflector panels only help behind radiators on external walls.** Most homes get more from the first two than the third.

I'm going to be careful here, because radiator advice online is full of big savings numbers with no source. I haven't found a solid, independent figure for what bleeding or reflectors save in pounds, so I won't invent one. What I can do is explain what each fix does, so you can tell whether it applies to your house.

## Bleeding radiators: fix cold patches, not your bill

Air gets trapped in the system over time. It collects at the top of a radiator, so the top stays cold while the bottom is warm. Smart Energy GB lists the signs as [a radiator that is cold at the top, slow to warm up or making gurgling noises](https://www.smartenergygb.org/smart-living/smart-energy-tips/how-to-bleed-a-radiator).

Bleeding won't lower your unit rate or shrink your boiler. What it does is let the whole radiator give out heat, so the room warms properly. That matters for money because the usual response to a cold room is to turn the thermostat up for the whole house. Fix the radiator and you may not need to.

How to do it, following the same Smart Energy GB guide:

1. Switch the heating off and let the radiator cool.
2. Put a towel and a small container underneath.
3. Open the bleed valve slowly, anticlockwise, with a radiator key or flathead screwdriver, until the hissing stops and water starts to flow.
4. Close it again.
5. Check your boiler pressure afterwards. Bleeding can drop it, and the guide says to follow your boiler manual if it needs topping up.

Two notes. If the radiator is hot at the top but cold at the bottom, that points to sludge or debris rather than air, and that is a job for a heating engineer. And if the whole radiator is cold, check the valve is turned on before you reach for the key.

## TRVs: stop heating rooms nobody is in

A thermostatic radiator valve (TRV) is the dial on the side of the radiator, usually numbered 0 to 6. It senses the room temperature and closes the valve when the room reaches the setting. The Energy Saving Trust advises [using the lowest setting that keeps the room comfortable](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/thermostats-and-heating-controls/).

This is the one that can actually cut your gas use, because it changes where the heat goes. A spare bedroom or a box room that nobody uses doesn't need to be the same temperature as the living room. Turn that TRV down and the boiler has less to do.

Two habits help:

- Don't turn the TRV to 6 to heat a room faster. The dial sets a target temperature, not the speed, so a higher number just means a warmer room in the end.
- **Turn rooms down rather than fully off.** A cold, unheated room is more likely to get condensation, so keep a little heat in.

The Energy Saving Trust says that [installing heating controls could save around £100 a year in Great Britain](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/thermostats-and-heating-controls/). Be aware that figure covers heating controls as a package (a programmer, a room thermostat and TRVs together), not TRVs alone. If you already have all three and use them, you won't get that again.

## Reflector panels: only on the right wall

Reflector panels, or foil, go behind a radiator and bounce heat back into the room instead of letting it soak into the wall. The Energy Saving Trust's answer is that [they can be worth it as a low-cost fix](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/radiator-reflector-panels/), but that it is not really worth adding them to radiators on internal walls. An internal wall has a warm room on the other side, so heat going into it isn't lost.

That leaves radiators on external walls, and particularly uninsulated ones. If you have solid walls with no insulation, a panel behind an external-wall radiator is the best case. If your walls are already insulated, there is less heat escaping for a panel to catch.

Savings from a panel are modest, so treat it as a cheap extra rather than a fix for a cold house.

## What is gas worth to you?

Here is how to put a price on it. On 27 October 2026 Flexible Octopus charged 7.90p per kWh for gas in Yorkshire (region M), plus a standing charge of 28.81p a day, according to [Octopus's product data](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/VAR-22-11-01/). Other regions and suppliers will differ.

So every 100 kWh of gas you don't burn is worth about £7.90, and every 1,000 kWh is worth about £79. Your bill shows how many kWh you used last winter. Heating the rooms you don't use less is where those kWh come from.

## What I would do, in order

| Fix | Cost | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Bleed the radiators with cold tops | Free (radiator key from a DIY shop is cheap) | Anyone with cold patches or slow-warming rooms |
| Turn TRVs down in unused rooms | Free | Anyone with TRVs fitted |
| Reflector panels | Low | Radiators on uninsulated external walls |
| Draught-proofing and insulation | Varies | Everyone, ahead of reflectors |

If you've only got ten minutes, bleed the radiators, then walk round and turn the TRVs down in the rooms you aren't using. Put reflectors last.

Reflectors are a small thing, so don't expect them to fix a cold house. If the cold is coming in rather than going out, [draught-proofing on a budget](/blog/draught-proofing-on-a-budget-uk/) usually does more.

## The short version

- Bleed any radiator that is cold at the top, then check boiler pressure.
- Use your TRVs to heat the rooms you are in, not every room equally.
- Reflectors are worth it on external walls, not on internal ones.
- Price your gas and use the [gas or electric tool](/gas-or-electric/) to see what a room costs to heat.
