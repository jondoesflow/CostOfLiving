Is triple glazing worth paying extra for over double glazing in the UK? **For most homes replacing windows, no, not on bill savings alone. On my worked example below, a triple glazed window saves about £3 a year of gas over a good double glazed one, so the extra cost can take 20 to 40 years to come back.**

There are good reasons to choose it anyway: a very cold or exposed house, a noisy road, or a new build or extension where you are buying windows regardless. But if someone is selling it to you as a way to cut your bills, do the sum first.

## What the experts say

Which? asked nine of its Trusted Traders double glazing installers whether triple glazing is worth the extra spend. In its [guide to choosing double glazed windows](https://www.which.co.uk/reviews/double-glazing/article/how-to-buy-double-glazing/choosing-double-glazed-windows-and-doors) (dated 16 September 2026), it reports that "the unanimous answer was: no", though they thought it may be worthwhile in certain circumstances. They said it's expensive, and much more expensive to replace. Which? suggests that if full triple glazing is too expensive, you could use it only in the rooms that get coldest.

The Federation of Master Builders' [guide to triple glazed windows](https://fmb.org.uk/homepicks/windows/triple-glazed-windows) is more positive about triple glazing in general, but agrees on timing. It says that in most cases, replacing double glazed windows early with triple glazing "wouldn't make financial or ecological sense", and that the sums are better for an extension or new build.

## How much better is triple glazing?

Window performance is measured as a U-value: how much heat escapes through a square metre for each degree of difference between inside and out. Lower is better.

| | U-value (W/m²K) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Limit for a replacement window in England | 1.4 (or Window Energy Rating band B) | Approved Document L, Table 4.2 |
| Window in the government's model new home | 1.2 | Approved Document L, Table 1.1 |
| Triple glazing | about 0.6 to 0.8 | Federation of Master Builders |

The first two rows come from [Approved Document L](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/conservation-of-fuel-and-power-approved-document-l), the official guidance on the energy rules in the Building Regulations for England. Any new double glazed window you buy today should already meet 1.4 (or band B), so that's the fair comparison, not your old windows.

## Worked example: what does triple glazing save?

This is my own rough sum, using these figures and assumptions:

- Window: one 900mm by 1,200mm window, about 1.08 square metres. That's the size the FMB uses in its price table.
- U-values: 1.4 for double glazing (the limit for a replacement window) and 0.8 for triple glazing (the less generous end of the FMB's range).
- Weather: about 2,060 heating degree days a year. That's the long-term average for 1991 to 2020, at a base temperature of 15.5°C, based on Met Office data, from the government's [Energy Trends weather tables](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/energy-trends-section-7-weather) (table 7.1), which I added up from its monthly figures.
- Boiler: 85% efficient, the figure the [gas or electric tool](/gas-or-electric/) uses.
- Gas price: the Flexible Octopus unit rate was between 7.8p and 8.2p per kWh across the regions on 18 November 2026, from the Octopus Energy API. I've used 8p.

The sum: the difference in U-value (0.6) times the window area (1.08) times the degree days (2,060) times 24 hours comes to about 32kWh of heat a year. A boiler at 85% needs about 38kWh of gas to make that, which at 8p is **about £3 a year for that window**.

A house with ten windows like that would save about £30 a year. If you heat with electricity, the saving is bigger, because each unit of heat costs more.

## And what does it cost?

The FMB says triple glazing costs on average 10 to 20 per cent more than double glazing, though elsewhere on the same page it says installation prices typically exceed double glazing by over 20 per cent. Its price table, dated November 2026, puts a 900mm by 1,200mm uPVC casement triple glazed window at £700.

If that £700 is 10 to 20 per cent more than the double glazed version, the extra for triple glazing is roughly £60 to £120 a window. At £3 a year, that's about 20 to 40 years to pay back the difference, or longer at the FMB's higher figure, assuming gas prices stay put, which they won't.

Two things make the real figure look worse, not better:

- Less free heat from the sun. The FMB's table gives triple glazing a g-value (how much solar heat gets through) of 0.46, against 0.78 for double glazing. On a sunny winter day, double glazing lets more free warmth in, which my sum ignores.
- Quotes vary a lot. Ask for the whole-window U-value or Window Energy Rating of both options on the same quote, and do the sum with your numbers.

## When triple glazing does make sense

- **You're building an extension or a new home.** You're buying windows anyway, so you only pay the difference, and the FMB says the sums are better there.
- **A cold or exposed spot.** The colder your winters, the more degree days, and the bigger the saving.
- **Noise.** Which? says triple glazing should make your home more sound-proof than double glazing, and one installer it spoke to said it can help on a very busy road or in a very cold part of the UK.
- **The coldest rooms only.** A north-facing bedroom might be worth triple glazing while the rest of the house gets good double glazing.

One practical check from the FMB: triple glazed windows are heavier and thicker, which makes them unsuitable for some relatively thin or unsupported walls. Your installer should check.

## Rules to know before you replace any windows

The [Planning Portal](https://www.planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/doors-and-windows/building-regulations) says replacement windows must meet the building regulations: a limit on heat loss, enough ventilation, and an opening at least as good for escape in a fire as the one replaced. If you use an installer registered with a competent person scheme, they can sign the work off and you get a certificate. Keep it somewhere safe.

On planning, the Planning Portal says new windows of a similar appearance to the originals usually don't need permission. A listed building needs listed building consent for any significant works, and conservation areas or an Article 4 direction can add restrictions. Leaseholders may need permission from their landlord or management company first.

Where windows can't meet the usual limit because of the need to keep a building's character, Approved Document L allows single glazing to be supplemented with low-emissivity secondary glazing instead.

## Cheaper things to do first

If your double glazing is working, the savings from replacing it are small. The heat you're losing is more likely to be through draughts:

- **Draught strip** on the frames of windows that open, and doors.
- **Thermal curtains** drawn at dusk.
- **Window film** on single glazed windows you won't open over winter.

I compared those, and secondary glazing, in my post on [thermal curtains, window film and secondary glazing for renters](/blog/thermal-curtains-window-film-secondary-glazing-renters/). The advice works just as well if you own your home.

## The short version

- Triple glazing loses less heat than double glazing, but a new double glazed window is already good.
- On my worked example, triple saves about £3 a year per window on gas at 8p per kWh, against an extra £60 to £120 a window. That's a 20 to 40 year payback.
- Which?'s installers said it isn't worth it for most homes. It can make sense for new builds, extensions, cold or noisy spots, or just the coldest room.
- Ask for the U-value or Window Energy Rating of each option on your quote.

Before you get a quote, look at what your heating actually costs. The [gas or electric comparison](/gas-or-electric/) shows how gas central heating compares with electric heating at today's prices, so you know whether you're chasing pennies or pounds.
