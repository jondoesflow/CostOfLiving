Is loft insulation worth it? **If your loft has little or none, yes, by a long way. If it already has a decent layer, a top-up saves far less than you might think.** The Energy Saving Trust's figures put insulating an empty loft at £140 to £360 a year for a gas-heated home, and topping up from 120mm at £13 to £35.

So the first job isn't buying anything. It's going up the ladder with a tape measure. Here are the numbers, the safety points I would not skip, and who can get it done for free.

## What it saves

These are the Energy Saving Trust's estimates for a gas-heated home, based on fuel prices in July 2026, as published by [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/reviews/insulation/article/how-to-buy-loft-insulation/loft-insulation-costs-and-savings-abthT7S8rcJT). I read them there on 9 November 2026, because the Trust's own website blocked my automated access that day.

| Type of home | Empty loft to 270mm, a year | 120mm to 270mm, a year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Detached house | £360 | £35 |
| Semi-detached house | £190 | £17 |
| Mid-terrace house | £140 | £13 |
| Detached bungalow | £350 | £35 |

The gap between the two columns is the whole story. Most of the benefit comes from the first layer. Once there is 120mm up there, going to 270mm still helps, but it is a small, steady saving rather than a big one.

These are averages for whole homes. Your own saving depends on the size of your loft, how warm you keep the house and what you pay for gas. They are a guide to whether the job is worth doing, not a promise.

## How much insulation should you have?

The recommended depth is 270mm. [Haringey Council's loft insulation guidance](https://haringey.gov.uk/housing/housing-emergencies/home-heat-loss/home-insulation/loft-insulation) says building regulations specify 270mm, and Which? gives the same figure.

To check yours, push a tape measure or a ruler down through the insulation between two joists until it touches the ceiling below. Do it in a couple of places, because older insulation is often squashed or patchy. Wear gloves, and stand on the joists or on boards, never between them, as the ceiling won't take your weight.

Roughly:

- **Nothing, or only a thin layer:** this is the big win from the table. Do it this winter, or apply for it free if you qualify (see below).
- **Around 100mm to 150mm:** worth topping up if it is easy to reach and you are doing it yourself, but don't expect big numbers.
- **270mm or more, and in good condition:** you're done. Look at draughts, the loft hatch and your heating controls instead.

## Can you do it yourself?

Often, yes. Haringey's guidance says mineral wool rolls are suitable for DIY, laid in two layers: one between the joists and one across them. Loose fill is different, as it needs specialist tools, so that is a job for a contractor.

On cost, Which? says a 100mm roll typically costs around £20 and covers 8.3 square metres. Measure your loft floor before you buy, and check the thickness and coverage printed on the roll you are looking at, because they vary.

The safety points matter more than the savings, so here they are from the same Haringey guidance:

- **Keep the eaves clear.** The openings where the roof meets the walls let air move through the loft, and Haringey says great care must be taken not to block them.
- **Don't cover downlights.** Leave space around recessed lights so they can't overheat. Ideally run electrical cables above the insulation, not buried under it.
- **Leave the area under a cold water tank uninsulated**, to reduce the risk of it freezing, and insulate the tank and its pipes instead. Once the loft is insulated it gets colder up there.
- **Insulate and draught-strip the loft hatch**, as a lot of heat escapes through it.
- **Wear protective clothing and a mask.** Mineral wool irritates skin and you don't want to breathe the fibres.
- **If you need to walk or store things up there**, don't squash the insulation with boards laid straight on top. Haringey suggests laying boards on extra timbers to make a safe walkway.

If your loft is awkward to get into, you have downlights everywhere, or you are not confident about the wiring, pay a professional. A cheap DIY job that blocks the ventilation can cost more than it saves.

## Can you get it for free?

Possibly, and it is worth checking before you spend anything. I covered the schemes in detail in my post on [who qualifies for free insulation](/blog/great-british-insulation-scheme-eco4-who-qualifies-how-to-apply/). In short, as at that post:

- **ECO4** runs until 31 December 2026, according to Ofgem. It mostly helps households on certain benefits, or people referred by their council through ECO4 Flex.
- **The Warm Homes: Local Grant** in England covers loft insulation for privately owned homes rated D to G on their Energy Performance Certificate, subject to income and your council having funding. You apply through [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/apply-warm-homes-local-grant).

If you might qualify, apply first. If you don't, a DIY top-up is one of the cheaper jobs on the list.

- Insulating an empty loft saves £140 to £360 a year for a gas-heated home, on the Energy Saving Trust's July 2026 figures.
- Topping up from 120mm to 270mm saves £13 to £35 a year. Worth doing cheaply, not worth a big bill.
