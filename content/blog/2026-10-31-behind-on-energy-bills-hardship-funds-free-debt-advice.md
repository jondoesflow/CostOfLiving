Short answer first: **if you are behind on your energy bills, contact your supplier before anything else and ask for a payment plan, a payment break and access to their hardship fund. Your supplier has to help you find a way to pay, and free debt advice is available alongside it.**

This is the post I'd want someone to read before they start avoiding the envelopes. Getting in touch early is the single most useful thing you can do, and it costs nothing.

Everything below comes from [Ofgem's page on getting help with your energy bills](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/your-energy-supply/your-energy-bill/get-help-your-home-or-business-energy-bills), [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/get-help-paying-your-bills/struggling-to-pay-your-energy-bills/) and the [government and Ofgem energy debt strategy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/energy-debt-circuit-breaker-a-strategy-for-domestic-energy-debt/energy-debt-circuit-breaker-our-joint-strategy-for-domestic-energy-debt), all of which I read on 31 October 2026. ## What to ask your supplier for

Ofgem says that if you are struggling to pay, or think you will be, you can ask your supplier to:

- agree a payment plan, payment break or reduction
- review your current payments and debt repayments
- give you access to financial support called "hardship funds"

Ofgem also suggests asking whether your supplier has any schemes or grants for energy costs, paying off energy debt, or making energy-saving improvements to your home. You can contact them online, in writing or by phone. The details should be on their website or your bill.

Citizens Advice is blunt that your supplier has to help you come to a solution, and that you should try to negotiate a deal that works for both of you. If you don't try, they might threaten to disconnect you.

## How a payment plan works

Citizens Advice explains that you pay fixed amounts over a set period, based on what you can afford. The plan covers what you owe plus an amount for your current use. Your supplier must take into account how much you can afford to pay, and how much energy you'll use in future.

Their worked example is a useful one. You owe £400 and tell your supplier the most you can manage is £40 a week. You agree to pay £10 a week towards the debt and £30 a week for current energy use, until the debt is cleared.

Two practical tips from the same page:

- Give your supplier details of your income, outgoings, debts and personal circumstances. If you aren't sure what you can afford, the free budget planner on MoneyHelper helps.
- Send regular meter readings, so the estimate of your future use is accurate.

## If you can't afford the plan

Go back and say so. Citizens Advice says you can ask your supplier to pause repayments for a short time, for example if you've lost your job or you're waiting for a benefit payment. They must consider your situation, but they don't have to agree. If you don't think they've considered it, you can complain.

Energy arrears are also a "priority debt", so they come before things like credit cards. If you have several debts, it's worth working out which to pay first with a free adviser.

## Paying through your benefits: the Fuel Direct Scheme

If you get certain benefits, your debt can be repaid straight from them. Citizens Advice lists Income-Based Jobseeker's Allowance, Income Support, income-related Employment and Support Allowance, Pension Credit and Universal Credit. If you get Universal Credit and work, you only qualify if your earnings are below your work allowance.

A fixed amount is taken automatically. Your supplier can ask for this without your permission, though they should try other ways to help you first. Citizens Advice says it's usually better than a prepayment meter, because you won't risk having no gas or electricity. If you do have a prepayment meter and can't afford to top up, Citizens Advice has separate advice on that.

## Free debt advice and breathing space

Ofgem points to [MoneyHelper](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/) for free information and advice on debts. It also says you can apply for temporary protection from your creditors, so they can't take action to recover what you owe while you get advice. In England and Wales this is called Breathing Space or the Debt Respite Scheme. In Scotland it's a debt moratorium.

For energy-specific help, Ofgem lists these free numbers:

| Where you live | Who to contact | Phone |
|---|---|---|
| England | Citizens Advice | 0808 144 8844 |
| Scotland | Energyadvice.scot | 0808 196 8660 |
| Wales | Advicelink Cymru | 0800 702 2020 |

Your local council may also offer support with energy bills, so it's worth looking at its website.

## What is the Energy Debt Circuit Breaker?

This was announced in the Budget on 28 October 2026. [Budget 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-document/budget-2026-html) says the government will introduce an Energy Debt Circuit Breaker. A joint strategy from the government and Ofgem was published the same day.

The strategy says energy debt in Great Britain has reached £5 billion (Q2 2026), and more than 2 million households are in energy debt and arrears. It is built on three ideas: relief, repayment and reform. That includes targeted, one-off debt relief for vulnerable households.

Here is the honest bit. **None of this is help you can claim today.** The strategy says it will launch Ofgem's Debt Relief Scheme "as soon as possible in 2027". I haven't found published eligibility rules for it, so I won't guess who will qualify. The strategy also says consumers remain responsible for paying their bills and making repayments, and that it wants suppliers to give early support.

The government's own analysis, in the Budget document, says the strategy could save a typical household almost £30 by 2030. That is a long-term estimate across all bill payers, not a payment into your account. If you're in debt now, the routes above are the ones that work this winter.

## A short checklist

1. Find your latest bill and your supplier's contact details.
2. Contact them and ask for a payment plan, a payment break or reduction, and their hardship fund.
3. Have your income, outgoings and a recent meter reading ready.
4. Ask whether the Fuel Direct Scheme is an option if you get a qualifying benefit.
5. Call the free number for your nation above, or use MoneyHelper, for debt advice.
6. If you aren't happy with how your supplier treated you, complain to them.

## Keep the bills you can control smaller

Debt advice deals with what you owe. Cutting what you use from now on helps too, and the cheapest wins are in timing. My post on [the cheapest time to use electricity in the UK](/blog/cheapest-time-to-use-electricity-uk/) is a good start, and the [Should I switch tool](/should-i-switch/) shows whether another tariff would suit your own usage. Just check your supplier's rules before switching while in debt.

Start with one thing today: pick up the phone, or write the email, and tell your supplier you need help. It's the step everything else depends on.
