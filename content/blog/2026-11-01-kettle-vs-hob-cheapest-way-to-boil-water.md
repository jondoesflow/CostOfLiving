Is it cheaper to boil a kettle or use the hob? **If you have an electric hob, the kettle wins, because a kettle puts nearly all its heat into the water and a pan on a hob does not. If you have a gas hob, gas is cheaper per unit, so it is much closer, and the biggest saving is boiling only what you need.**

A litre of water through an electric kettle costs roughly 3p on today's prices. That is small, which is why the habit matters more than the appliance. Below is the sum, with every assumption shown so you can swap in your own numbers.

## The prices I used

These are the Flexible Octopus standard rates for London (region C), including VAT, from the Octopus API on 1 November 2026:

| Fuel | Unit rate |
|---|---|
| Electricity | 26.6p per kWh |
| Gas | 8.15p per kWh |

Your region and supplier will differ a little. The [Cheap Watts](/) calculator uses live prices if you want your own figure.

## How much energy does it take to boil water?

This part is physics, not opinion. It takes about 4.186 kilojoules to raise 1 kg of water by 1°C, and a litre of water weighs 1 kg. (Source: [the US National Institute of Standards and Technology's reference value for water](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C7732185&Mask=2).)

Heating 1 litre from 15°C to 100°C is a rise of 85°C:

- 4.186 x 85 = about 356 kilojoules
- 3,600 kilojoules make a kilowatt hour, so that is **0.099 kWh**

The 15°C starting temperature is my assumption. Your cold tap will be warmer in summer and colder in winter, so the real figure moves a little.

That 0.099 kWh is the least energy any method can use. Everything else is waste.

## The kettle sum

I assumed an electric kettle turns about 90% of its electricity into heat in the water. That is my working assumption, not a tested figure for your kettle, so treat it as a sensible starting point. A kettle's element sits inside the water, so most of the heat has nowhere else to go.

- 0.099 kWh divided by 0.9 = 0.11 kWh of electricity
- 0.11 kWh x 26.6p = **about 2.9p per litre**

## The hob sum

A hob has to heat the pan, the air around the pan and the kitchen before the water gets its share. I do not have a sourced efficiency figure for hobs, so I will not pretend to give you one. Instead, here is what each type would need to achieve.

**Electric hob.** Electricity costs the same per unit however you use it, so the hob only wins if it wastes less heat than the kettle. A pan on a ring generally loses more, so the kettle is the safer bet. If you have an induction hob, which heats the pan directly, the gap is likely to be smaller, but I have no verified figure to give you.

**Gas hob.** Gas costs about a third of electricity per kWh (8.15p against 26.6p). That changes the sum. Work out the cost per kWh of heat that reaches the water:

- Kettle: 26.6p divided by 0.9 = about 29.6p per useful kWh
- Gas hob: 8.15p divided by its efficiency

Those two are equal when the gas hob's efficiency is about 28%. So the kettle only comes out cheaper if the hob and pan waste more than roughly 72% of the gas. As an illustration only, if a gas hob delivered 50% of its heat to the water, a litre would cost about 1.6p, against 2.9p for the kettle.

I would not read that as "always use gas". Boiling in an open pan can lose a lot of heat, and the pan takes longer. It does show that on a gas hob the two methods are close enough that convenience can decide it. If you have a gas hob and mostly want a quick cup of tea, the kettle is still fine.

## The habit that saves more than the choice of appliance

The biggest saving is not which method you use. It is how much water you heat.

Suppose you fill the kettle with 500ml more than you need, five times a day. That extra half litre costs:

- 0.5 litre x 85°C x 4.186 / 3,600 = 0.049 kWh of water heating
- Divided by 0.9 and multiplied by 26.6p = about 1.5p per boil
- 1.5p x 5 boils x 365 days = **about £27 a year**

Those numbers are assumptions (five boils a day, an extra 500ml each time), so your total could be higher or lower. But it shows the scale: a small habit adds up to more than the argument about kettle versus hob.

Practical ways to cut it:

1. Fill the kettle for the cups you are making, not to the top. Many kettles have a minimum fill line, so check yours.
2. Boil once and fill a flask if several people want a drink.
3. For pasta or veg, boil the kettle first and tip the water into the pan. It is quick, and on an electric hob it avoids the slower, wasteful heating of a cold pan. Put a lid on the pan to hold the heat in.
4. Descale the kettle if the element is crusted over. I have not found a sourced figure for how much this saves, so I will not give you one, but a clean kettle is no worse.

## What to do next

Look at your kettle tonight. Fill it with one mug of water and see where the water level sits against the markings. If you usually fill it much higher than that, your habit is the thing to change.
