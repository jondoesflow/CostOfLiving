Short answer: **for washing machines, tumble dryers and dishwashers, no. The government's own fire safety advice says plainly: "Don't run appliances such as washing machines, tumble dryers and dishwashers overnight." The same goes for charging e-bike and e-scooter batteries while you're asleep.**

That's awkward, because the cheapest electricity on many tariffs is in the middle of the night. On Octopus Go in Yorkshire on 25 November 2026, the cheap rate was 9.40p per kWh from 12.30am to 5.30am, against 33.06p the rest of the day. That's a big gap, and I understand why people are tempted.

So here's what the fire services actually say, what is designed to run overnight, what the safe option costs you, and how to get most of the saving without gambling on it.

## What the fire services say

The government's [Fire safety in the home](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/make-your-home-safe-from-fire/fire-safety-in-the-home-accessible-version) guide is short and worth reading in full. On appliances, it says:

- "Don't run appliances such as washing machines, tumble dryers and dishwashers overnight."
- "Unplug appliances when you're not using them or when you go to bed."
- "When charging electrical goods, follow the manufacturer's instructions."

[Devon and Somerset Fire and Rescue](https://www.dsfire.gov.uk/news/safety/why-you-shouldnt-use-washing-machines-and-tumble-dryers-night) explains why. If an appliance starts a fire at night, "the fire may have more time to develop before you're aware of it." Its advice is simple: "The safest time to use electricals is when you are awake, and definitely in the house." When it published that advice in 2022, it said it attends "around 115 fires each year caused by domestic appliances", and that's one fire service area.

For e-bikes and e-scooters, [London Fire Brigade](https://www.london-fire.gov.uk/safety/e-bikes-and-e-scooters/how-to-charge-e-bikes-and-e-scooters-safely/) is even more direct: "Never leave a battery charging unattended or while you're asleep."

None of this is new, and none of it changes because your tariff is cheap at 2am.

## What is designed to run overnight

Some things are meant to use night-time electricity, and they're a different case from a washing machine on a timer.

- **Storage heaters.** [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/save-energy-at-home/using-storage-heaters-efficiently/) explains they're "directly connected to your off-peak electricity supply" and charge up with heat during off-peak times. That's the whole point of them.
- **Immersion heaters on an off-peak timer.** The same Citizens Advice page says that if yours has a timer, "you should set it to come on during off-peak times."
- **Electric blankets, but only some.** The government guide says to unplug a blanket before you get into bed, "unless it has a thermostat control for safe all-night use." Check yours before you leave it on.
- **Fridges and freezers**, which run all the time anyway.

I haven't covered home electric car chargers here. That's a separate subject, so follow the instructions from your charger's maker and installer.

## What the safe option costs

Here's the honest bit. Running things while you're awake does cost more on a tariff like Go. These are the site's typical figures, with Yorkshire rates on 25 November 2026.

| Appliance (typical use) | Go, cheap hours (9.40p) | Go, daytime (33.06p) | Flexible Octopus, Direct Debit (25.60p) | Cosy Octopus, 1pm to 4pm (13.59p) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Washing machine, 40°C (0.8 kWh) | 7.5p | 26.4p | 20.5p | 10.9p |
| Dishwasher, eco (1.0 kWh) | 9.4p | 33.1p | 25.6p | 13.6p |
| Tumble dryer, full load (4 kWh) | 37.6p | £1.32 | £1.02 | 54.4p |

On Go, a dishwasher run in the day rather than at night costs about 24p more. For a tumble dryer it's about 95p a load. Over a year of typical use (four washes, five dishwasher runs and three dryer loads a week), running all three in Go's daytime rather than its night hours adds up to about £248.

That's real money. It's also the price of not having a hot, spinning appliance running in a sleeping house. I'd rather you knew the number and made the call with your eyes open than pretended it was zero.

## How to get most of the saving safely

### 1. Look for cheap hours when you're awake

Not every cheap window is in the middle of the night. On Cosy Octopus in Yorkshire on 25 November 2026, the cheap rate of 13.59p applied from 1pm to 4pm as well as 4am to 7am and 10pm to midnight. If someone's home in the afternoon, a dishwasher in that window cost 13.6p, not far off Go's night rate. Octopus describes Cosy as a heat pump tariff, so check whether you qualify.

[Cheap Watts](/) finds the cheapest time to run each appliance on your tariff, and you can pick a slot when you're up and about. I covered how the price changes through the day in [What's the cheapest time to use electricity in the UK?](/blog/cheapest-time-to-use-electricity-uk/)

### 2. Cut what goes in the machine

Devon and Somerset Fire and Rescue's own money-saving tips are about doing less, not doing it at night: "Make sure your washing machine is full before you do a load," and "could you air dry rather than using the tumble dryer?" One fewer dryer load a week saves more than moving it to a cheaper hour.

### 3. Run the big one first

The tumble dryer is where most of the money is. If you can only fit one appliance into a cheap daytime window, make it that one.

### 4. Keep the machines themselves safe

The same fire service says: "Make sure you keep the lint filters on tumble dryers super clean." It's also worth checking whether your appliance has been recalled. [Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/product-recalls/) and [Register My Appliance](https://www.registermyappliance.org.uk/) both list recalls, and registering your appliance is a way to hear about safety repairs or recalls.

## If you do run something overnight anyway

I'd rather you didn't. But if you're going to, Devon and Somerset Fire and Rescue gives two steps for when night use can't be avoided:

- Make sure all your smoke alarms are working, and if possible consider one near the appliance.
- "Close all doors that lead to the room with the appliance you're using in."

It also points out that kitchens shouldn't have smoke alarms; use a heat alarm there instead.

The government guide says to "fit at least one smoke alarm on every level of your home" and "test your smoke alarms at least monthly". It also says you're "around 8 times more likely to die in a fire if you do not have a working smoke alarm in your home." If you charge an e-bike or e-scooter at home, London Fire Brigade says to "fit a smoke alarm in the area where you charge your batteries", charge on a hard, flat surface and unplug the charger once it's finished.
