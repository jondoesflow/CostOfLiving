What uses the most electricity in a UK home? **Anything that makes heat.** On the typical figures Cheap Watts uses, the biggest users are an electric car, an immersion heater, plug-in electric heaters, a tumble dryer and an electric shower. At 25.60p per kWh on 17 November 2026, each of those can cost well over £100 a year. A washing machine, an air fryer or a phone charger barely registers next to them.

The rule of thumb is simple. Motors and screens are cheap. Heating water, air or clothes is expensive. So if you want to know where your money goes, start with the things that get hot.

A year is 52 weeks here. Your own numbers will be different. A shower once a day is per person, so a family of four could easily use four times that figure. Somebody who tumble dries every day will spend more than twice what's shown.

Don't add the column up and treat it as a typical bill. Hardly anyone has every item on the list, and plenty of homes have a combi boiler instead of an immersion heater, or a gas hob instead of an electric oven.

## Why heat costs so much

It comes down to watts. A kettle, a shower or a heater pulls thousands of watts while it's on. A laptop charger pulls tens.

An electric shower at 9.5kW uses more power in eight minutes than an electric blanket does in nearly 13 hours. A 2kW heater uses 2 kWh every hour it runs, so a cold evening of four hours is 8 kWh, or about £2.05 at 25.60p.

That's why a washing machine sits so low in the table. Most of its electricity goes on heating the water at the start. The rest is a motor turning a drum, and that's cheap. A tumble dryer is near the top for the same reason in reverse: it spends the whole cycle making hot air.

## The ones that run all the time

Some things never make the list above because they're never switched off, so nobody thinks to count them. Fridges and freezers run day and night. So do routers, and anything sitting on standby.

On their own, most of these are small. Together they add up, because they run for 8,760 hours a year. A device drawing 10W all year costs about £22. I worked through the sum, and which gadgets are worth switching off, in [Which gadgets cost the most on standby?](/blog/standby-power-cost-uk-switch-off-at-the-wall/)

For your fridge or freezer, the energy label gives a figure in kWh a year, and I explained how to read it in [Energy labels explained](/blog/energy-labels-explained-a-to-g-pounds-per-year/). Multiply it by your unit rate and you have a rough yearly cost. If it's an old one, a plug-in monitor will tell you what it really uses over a few days.

## What about heating and hot water?

If your home has gas central heating and a combi boiler, your biggest energy cost is probably gas, not electricity, and it won't show in this list at all.

If you heat with electricity, everything changes. Plug-in heaters, storage heaters and an immersion heater are likely to outweigh every other appliance. The electric heater row above is only ten hours a week. Run one for six hours a day through winter and the cost climbs fast.

## Where to cut first

Start at the top of the table and work down. A few pence saved on a big user beats pounds of effort on a small one.

1. **Move what you can to cheaper hours.** On a time-of-use tariff, an electric car, an immersion heater and a dishwasher are the easiest things to shift. An immersion timer that heats the tank only in your cheap window saves you paying the day rate for hot water you won't use until the evening.
2. **Dry clothes differently.** On these figures, a heat pump dryer uses 390 kWh a year less than a condenser or vented dryer for the same three loads a week. That's about £100 a year at 25.60p. A heated airer saves a little more, at about £112, if you have the room and the patience.
3. **Shorten showers.** Every minute of a 9.5kW electric shower costs about 4p. Two minutes less, once a day, is about £30 a year per person.
4. **Heat the person, not the room.** An electric blanket costs about 3p to warm a bed. A 2kW heater costs about £1.02 for two hours.
5. **Then deal with the always-on items.** Switch off anything big on standby and check the age of your fridge and freezer.

## How to find your own biggest users

The table is a starting point. Two things will show you what's really happening at home.

**Your smart meter data.** Many suppliers show your usage by the half hour in their app. Look for the spikes. If your use jumps every evening at the same time, that's usually the shower, the oven or a heater. There's more on finding and downloading it in [How do I read my smart meter?](/blog/read-smart-meter-download-half-hourly-usage/)

**A plug-in energy monitor.** Put it between the socket and an appliance and it counts the kWh. Leave it on the fridge for a week, or run one dryer load through it, and you'll have a real number instead of a typical one.

Then use the [Cheap Watts home page](/) to pick any appliance from the list, put in your own figures, and see what it costs to run at different times of day on today's prices.
