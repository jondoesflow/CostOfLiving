Short answer: **yes, it can pay, but the saving is smaller than most people expect. At the prices I checked on 12 November 2026, a 5 kWh battery charged overnight and used during the day saves roughly £113 to £137 a year against staying on a flat tariff. Whether that is worth having depends on what the battery costs you.**

A home battery doesn't make electricity. It moves it. You buy cheap units in the middle of the night, and use them later when the price is higher.

So the whole question is how big the gap is between the two prices, and how much of it survives the trip through the battery.

## The sum, in one line

Saving on each unit you get back = the price you avoid, minus the night price divided by the battery's efficiency.

That last bit matters. You never get out everything you put in. The Cheap Watts battery calculator assumes 90% round trip, so 5 kWh in gives you 4.5 kWh back. Check the figure for your own battery and swap it in.

Here is what that does to a night rate. Buy at 16.87p, lose 10% on the way, and each unit you use has cost you 18.74p.

## What the night rates look like

These are the rates in the supplier price data for Yorkshire, paying by Direct Debit, on 12 November 2026.

| Tariff | Cheap hours | Cheap rate | 4pm to 7pm | Rest of the day | Standing charge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flexible Octopus (flat) | none | 25.60p | 25.60p | 25.60p | 60.89p a day |
| EDF FreePhase Static | 11pm to 6am | 16.87p | 45.68p | 25.25p | 54.81p a day |
| Octopus Flux | 2am to 5am | 15.56p | 35.99p | 25.78p | 60.86p a day |
| Octopus Go | 12.30am to 5.30am | 9.40p | 33.06p | 33.06p | 60.86p a day |

Who can have them, going by the suppliers' own descriptions in that data:

- **FreePhase Static** is fixed for 24 months, with a £100 exit fee per fuel.
- **Octopus Flux** is "designed exclusively for solar and battery owners".
- **Octopus Go** is described as an electric car tariff. I've included it to show what a wider price gap does.

All of them need a smart meter. Eligibility rules change, so read the supplier's page before you plan around one.

## A worked example: a 5 kWh battery

I've used the calculator's starting figures: a 5 kWh battery that charges at 3kW and gives back 90%. Filling it takes about 1 hour 40 minutes, which fits inside every cheap window above.

The same 4.5 kWh bought on the flat tariff would cost £1.15.

| Tariff | Cost to fill it | Cost of each unit you get back | Saved against the flat tariff, per day | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EDF FreePhase Static | 84p | 18.74p | 31p | £113 |
| Octopus Flux | 78p | 17.29p | 37p | £137 |
| Octopus Go | 47p | 10.44p | 68p | £249 |

Those yearly figures assume four things:

- you fill and empty the battery every single day
- your home uses at least 4.5 kWh outside the cheap hours each day
- the battery covers your 4pm to 7pm use first
- prices stay where they are, and they won't

Miss any of those and the number drops.

## Why a calculator can show a bigger number

Put the FreePhase Static rates into a battery calculator and you may see a much larger saving: about £1.21 a charge, if your home uses the whole charge at teatime. That is 4.5 kWh at the 45.68p teatime price, less the 84p it cost to fill.

Both sums are right. They answer different questions.

The £1.21 compares the battery with buying at the teatime peak on the same tariff. The 31p compares it with the flat tariff you could have stayed on. A lot of what the battery "saves" on a time-of-use tariff is the dear teatime price that the tariff itself brought in.

If you are deciding whether to buy a battery at all, the flat tariff comparison is the honest one.

## The catches

**The dear hours apply to everything the battery doesn't cover.** On Octopus Go, every daytime unit costs 7.45p more than the flat rate. If your home uses about 9 kWh a day beyond what the battery supplies, that extra cost wipes out the 68p. On FreePhase Static, any teatime unit the battery can't cover costs 20.08p more than flat.

**Batteries fade.** Capacity drops with age and use. Ask what the warranty promises, in years and in cycles, and what capacity it guarantees at the end.

**Tie-ins.** A 24 month fix with an exit fee is a real commitment. Flux and Go are variable, so their rates can change.

**Half-hourly tariffs are a different bet.** On Agile Octopus, from 4 to 11 November 2026, the cheapest two hours before 4pm averaged between 28.3p and 34.3p in Yorkshire. That is above the 25.60p flat rate before any losses. A battery would only have protected you from Agile's own teatime peak. There are much cheaper weeks too, and I covered both sides in [Octopus Agile explained](/blog/octopus-agile-explained-who-it-suits/).

## How long would it take to pay back?

I can't give you a verified price for a fitted battery, so here is the sum to run on your own quotes. Divide the full installed price by the yearly saving.

| Yearly saving | Years to pay back each £1,000 of cost |
|---|---|
| £113 | 8.8 |
| £137 | 7.3 |
| £249 | 4.0 |

So a quote of £3,000 with a £137 saving takes about 22 years. That is before allowing for the battery fading. Then hold the answer against the length of the warranty.

On night charging alone, with a battery this size, the sums are tight. A bigger battery saves more, but only if your home can use what it stores every day.

## What about a portable power station?

The same sum applies, with smaller numbers. Take a 1 kWh unit, and assume it also gives back 90%. Filled at 16.87p and used in place of flat rate electricity, it saves about 6p a day, or roughly £23 a year if you cycle it daily.

It also only runs what you plug into it. It doesn't feed the house wiring.

They are useful things to own for camping or a power cut. I wouldn't buy one to cut the bill.

## Where solar changes the answer

With panels on the roof, the battery has a second job: storing the afternoon surplus for the evening. That costs nothing to fill, so each unit is worth more than one bought at night.

Grid charging then becomes the winter job, for the months when the panels make little. A 4kW system in Leeds makes about 129 kWh in December against 452 kWh in June. The sums are in [Solar panels in the UK: how much do they generate?](/blog/solar-panels-uk-how-much-generate-use-or-sell/)

Flux adds one more option. It paid 29.18p for exports between 4pm and 7pm on 12 November 2026, so anything the house doesn't need at teatime can be sold for more than the 17.29p it cost to store.

## Safety

A fixed home battery is an electrical installation, and a job for a qualified installer.

There is a fire safety specification for fitting them in homes: [PAS 63100](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/electrical-installations-protection-against-fire-of-battery-energy-storage-systems-for-use-in-dwellings-specification), published by BSI in March 2024. It covers where the battery goes, among other things. Ask your installer whether they work to it, and where in the house they plan to put the battery.

A [government review of domestic battery safety](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/domestic-battery-energy-storage-systems) found few recorded fires involving home lithium-ion storage systems, but it is clear that a large battery in a home is a hazard that needs managing properly.

## What to do next

1. **Find your daily use outside the cheap hours.** Your smart meter's half-hourly data shows it. If it is well under the battery's size, you won't use a full charge.
2. **Run today's prices.** The [solar and batteries tool](/solar-and-batteries/) takes your battery's size, charge speed and efficiency, and shows the best time to charge and what one charge is worth.
3. **Do the flat tariff sum too.** Units you get back, times your flat rate, minus the cost to fill.
4. **Divide the quote by the yearly saving.** Compare the answer with the warranty.

Start with step 1. If your home doesn't use the battery's worth of electricity outside the cheap hours, every figure above shrinks.
