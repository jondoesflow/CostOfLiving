Where does the heat escape that you can't see? **Three common places are the loft hatch, the gaps between floorboards and skirting, and the letterbox. Each is a cheap DIY fix: draught-strip and insulate the hatch, seal the gaps around the floor edge, and fit a brush or flap on the letterbox. None needs a tradesperson, and none should block the ventilation your home needs.**

I covered the obvious gaps round doors and windows in my post on [draught-proofing on a budget](/blog/draught-proofing-on-a-budget-uk/). This one is for the leaks that don't announce themselves.

## Why these three get missed

You feel a draught under a door. You don't feel warm air drifting up through a hatch into a cold loft, because it happens above your head. Warm air rises, so a gap near the ceiling lets it out and pulls cold air in lower down. That is the reason the loft hatch deserves a look even if you have already topped up your loft insulation.

## The loft hatch

The hatch is usually a thin board in a gap between warm rooms and a cold loft. In my post on [topping up loft insulation](/blog/loft-insulation-is-topping-it-up-worth-it/) I noted that a lot of heat escapes through it, and that it is worth insulating and draught-stripping. Here is the job:

1. **Fit self-adhesive draught strip** around the frame where the hatch closes, so it sits tight when shut. Clean the surface first so it sticks.
2. **Add insulation to the hatch itself.** Stick a piece of rigid insulation board to the loft side, cut to size.
3. **Keep a catch or bolt** so the hatch pulls down onto the seal rather than just resting on it.

Take care in the loft. Stay on the joists, not the plasterboard between them, and take a torch.

## Floorboards and skirting boards

Gaps between old floorboards and along the skirting let cold air up from the void below. They matter most on a ground floor with a suspended timber floor.

- **Skirting edge:** run a bead of flexible sealant along the gap between the skirting and the floor.
- **Wide gaps between boards:** flexible filler or strips made for the job. Use something that can move, as boards flex.
- **Cover what you can't fill:** a rug on top of bare boards helps with comfort, though it won't seal anything.

One firm warning. If your home has a suspended timber floor, the air bricks in the outside wall are there to ventilate the void under it, so leave them clear. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/carbon-monoxide-poisoning/) also advises keeping rooms well ventilated and flues clean where there are fuel-burning appliances, so don't seal around a chimney or boiler flue.

## The letterbox

A letterbox is a hole straight through your front door. A brush insert or a spring flap on the inside stops the draught and still lets the post through. A brush is the simpler fix and is cheap. If you want to stop post being pushed in at all, a letter cage on the inside is another option. Check the fit before you stick or screw anything in place.

## Check your work

- **Do the hand test again** on a cold, breezy day. Cold moving air at the hatch edge or letterbox means the seal isn't tight.
- **Watch for damp.** If condensation or mould appears after sealing, you have probably cut off ventilation somewhere. Open a window for a few minutes a day, and check any air bricks are clear.
- **Keep a carbon monoxide alarm** in rooms with a gas or solid fuel appliance, and have appliances serviced by a registered engineer.

## What to do this weekend

Pull the loft hatch shut and look for daylight round the edge. Run your hand round it, then along the skirting in the coldest ground-floor room, then across the back of the front door. Write down what you find, buy strip and a letterbox brush, and do the hatch first. Leave vents, air bricks and flues exactly as they are.
