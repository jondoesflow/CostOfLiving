Is it cheaper to warm one room with an electric heater than to put the central heating on? **On gas central heating, usually not. A unit of heat from a gas boiler costs about a third of a unit of heat from a plug-in heater, so the heater only wins when the central heating would be warming roughly three times as much space as you actually need.**

That does happen. If you live alone in a three-bed house and only ever sit in one room, the heater can come out ahead. But for most people the cheaper answer is the central heating with the empty rooms turned down. Here is the sum, so you can see which side of the line you are on.

## The prices I used

These are the Flexible Octopus standard rates for London (region C), including VAT, from the [Octopus API](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/VAR-22-11-01/) on 14 November 2026:

| Fuel | Unit rate | Standing charge |
|---|---|---|
| Electricity | 26.6p per kWh | 41.39p a day |
| Gas | 8.15p per kWh | 29.24p a day |

Your region and supplier will be a little different. The [gas or electric](/gas-or-electric/) tool works it out with your own prices.

## Cost per unit of heat

An electric heater turns all of its electricity into heat in the room, so a kWh of heat costs whatever a kWh of electricity costs you.

A gas boiler loses some heat up the flue. The Cheap Watts tools assume a boiler is about 85% efficient, which is a working figure, not a measurement of your boiler. On that basis:

| Heat source | Cost per kWh of heat |
|---|---|
| Plug-in electric heater | 26.6p |
| Gas boiler (8.15p divided by 0.85) | about 9.6p |

So heat from the plug-in heater costs about 2.8 times as much as heat from the boiler. That one number decides the whole question.

## Why it isn't that simple: the boiler heats more than one room

A heater warms the room it's in. Central heating warms every room with a radiator turned on, plus the pipes in between. You are paying for all of that heat, not just the bit that reaches your sofa.

So the fair comparison is not "one room against one room". It is "one room from the heater against however much of the house the central heating warms". Put simply:

- If the central heating would deliver **less than about 2.8 times** the heat your one room needs, the central heating is cheaper.
- If it would deliver **more than about 2.8 times** that heat, the heater is cheaper.

Another way to say it: if the room you are sitting in takes more than about a third of the heat your system puts out, stick with the central heating.

## A worked example

I'll use the figure the [gas or electric](/gas-or-electric/) tool uses for heating one room: about 1.5kW of heat. Say you want it for three hours on a winter evening. That is 4.5 kWh of heat.

- **Plug-in heater:** 4.5 kWh x 26.6p = **about £1.20**
- **Central heating, if it only warmed that room:** 4.5 kWh x 9.6p = **about 43p**

Now the honest bit. The central heating won't only warm that room. I don't know how much heat your house needs, so the next lines are illustrations I have made up to show where the line falls, not measurements:

| If the central heating delivers... | Heat | Gas cost | Cheaper option |
|---|---|---|---|
| Twice what your room needs | 9 kWh | about 86p | Central heating |
| About 2.8 times | 12.5 kWh | about £1.20 | A draw |
| Four times | 18 kWh | about £1.73 | Plug-in heater |

The pattern is the point. A small flat where you use most of the rooms sits near the top of that table. A big house where one person uses one room sits near the bottom.

## The option most people miss: central heating, turned down elsewhere

You don't have to choose between "the whole house" and "one room with a plug-in heater". If your radiators have thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs), the dials on the side, you can turn down the ones in rooms nobody is using and close those doors. That moves the central heating up the table above, because less of its heat goes to empty rooms.

I covered how TRVs work, and what to set them to, in my post on [bleeding radiators, reflector panels and TRVs](/blog/bleeding-radiators-reflector-panels-trvs-what-helps/). If your radiators don't have them, get a quote for fitting them before you assume it's worth it.

This is usually the cheapest way to warm the room you're in, because you get gas-priced heat without paying to heat the spare bedroom.

## When a plug-in heater does make sense

There are times when the heater is the right call:

- **You live alone in a bigger home** and only use one room in the evening.
- **You want ten minutes of warmth**, say in a cold bathroom or a home office first thing, and don't want to fire up the whole system.
- **You don't have gas central heating** at all. Then the comparison is with your storage heaters or other electric heating, which is a different sum.
- **Your electricity is very cheap at that time.** On a time-of-use tariff, a heater running when your rate is below about 9.6p a kWh matches gas on cost per unit of heat. The [Cheap Watts tool](/) shows when your cheapest hours are.

One thing that doesn't change the sum: the standing charges. If you have gas, you pay the gas standing charge every day whether the boiler runs or not, so it isn't saved by using an electric heater instead.

## Don't leave the rest of the house too cold

Saving money by heating one room is fine for the room you're in, but the NHS advises heating your home to a temperature that's comfortable for you and, if you can, [at least 18°C in the rooms you regularly use, such as your living room and bedroom](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/seasonal-health/keep-warm-keep-well/). They say this is particularly important if you have a health condition.

So if you heat the living room with a plug-in heater and go to bed in a cold bedroom, you haven't really solved the problem. Take the bedroom into account when you do the sum.

## Using a plug-in heater safely

Electrical Safety First's [portable heaters guidance](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/safety-advice/products-and-appliances/heating-cooling/portable-heaters/) is worth a read before you rely on one. The key points:

- Keep it at least one metre from furniture, curtains and anything else that can burn.
- Plug it straight into a wall socket, not an extension lead.
- Never dry clothes on it, and don't leave it running unattended or overnight.

## How to find out which is cheaper in your home

You don't have to guess. Try this over two similar evenings:

1. **Evening one:** central heating on as you normally have it, with the TRVs in empty rooms turned down. If you have a smart meter, note the gas use for that evening from your in-home display or supplier app.
2. **Evening two:** central heating off, plug-in heater on for the same hours. A smart plug with energy monitoring tells you exactly how many kWh it used.
3. **Compare the costs** using your own unit rates, and check the room got just as warm. A cheap room thermometer stops you fooling yourself.

Weather changes from night to night, so don't treat one pair of evenings as the final answer. But it will tell you which side of the line you are on, which is more than any general rule can. Then put your own prices into the [gas or electric](/gas-or-electric/) tool to see what a winter of it adds up to.
