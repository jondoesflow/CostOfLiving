How do you cut the cost of hot water? Use less of it, and heat what you do use at the cheapest rate you can. The biggest lever is shower time. An eight-minute electric shower costs about 33p at the current price cap, and a four-minute one costs about 17p. Do that once a day and the difference is about £61 a year, for one person.

Below are the sums for each common fix, with my assumptions stated so you can swap in your own.

## The prices I've used

I've used the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, Great Britain average on Direct Debit: 26.32p per kWh for electricity and 7.97p per kWh for gas ([Ofgem's announcement](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/news/changes-energy-price-cap-between-1-october-and-31-december-2026), checked on 7 November 2026). Your own rates are on your bill. The next cap is due on 25 November 2026, so these will change from 1 January.

The electric shower and immersion figures are the typical ones Cheap Watts uses: a 9.5kW shower uses about 1.27 kWh in 8 minutes, and a 3kW immersion heater uses 3 kWh an hour.

## Electric showers: time is the whole story

An electric shower heats the water as it flows, so every minute costs the same.

| Shower | Energy | Cost each | Cost per year, one a day |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 minutes | 1.27 kWh | 33p | £122 |
| 4 minutes | 0.63 kWh | 17p | £61 |

So a timer in the bathroom is worth having. Four minutes is a target, not a rule: even two minutes off an eight-minute shower saves about £30 a year per daily shower.

The Energy Saving Trust gives a similar figure for gas-heated homes: it says a four-minute shower can save about £45 a year for an average household ([Energy Saving Trust bathroom tips](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/top-tips-to-save-energy-and-money-in-the-bathroom/)). Its assumptions differ from mine, so the numbers won't match exactly.

## Gas showers: what a low-flow shower head can save

If your shower runs off a gas boiler, the cost is the gas used to heat the water. This is where the shower head matters, because a lower flow rate means less water to heat.

I can't give you a measured saving, because flow rates vary a lot between homes. Here is a worked example with my own assumptions, which you should treat as illustrative:

- A four-minute shower, once a day.
- Water heated by about 28°C on its way to the shower.
- A boiler that turns 90% of its gas into heat.
- Heating one litre of water by one degree takes 0.001163 kWh (standard physics).

| Flow rate | Water per shower | Gas per shower | Cost each | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 litres a minute | 48 litres | 1.74 kWh | 13.8p | £50.52 |
| 8 litres a minute | 32 litres | 1.16 kWh | 9.2p | £33.68 |

That is a saving of about £17 a year for one daily shower. It scales with the number of showers in the house, so a family of four might see about four times that. A low-flow head that costs a few pounds pays back in a year or less on those numbers, but only if the shower still feels good enough that nobody stays in longer. If the lower flow makes people take longer showers, you lose the saving.

You can find your current flow rate by timing how long the shower takes to fill a 10-litre bucket: 50 seconds means 12 litres a minute.

## Immersion heaters: the timer is about the price, not the amount

If you have a hot water cylinder with an immersion heater, a timer doesn't reduce how much energy you use. It moves the heating to a cheaper time.

One hour of a 3kW element is 3 kWh, which costs about 79p at 26.32p per kWh. Do that every day and it comes to 1,095 kWh and about £288 a year.

What a timer can change is the rate. Every 1p per kWh you save by heating in cheap hours takes about £11 a year off that 1,095 kWh. If you are on a tariff with a cheaper night rate such as Economy 7, set the timer for those hours; I explain how to find them in [my Economy 7 post](/blog/economy-7-off-peak-hours-how-to-find-yours/). Check your own off-peak rate on your bill rather than assuming one.

Two practical points:

1. Heat the cylinder only as long as you need. If it's already hot, the thermostat cuts the element off, but a timer that runs for hours you don't need just invites standing losses.
2. A jacket on the cylinder cuts how fast the heat leaks away. I covered the sums in [lagging pipes and cylinder jackets](/blog/lagging-pipes-hot-water-cylinder-jacket-diy/).

## What about combi boilers?

A combi heats water only when you turn a tap on, so there is no tank to keep warm. That means the fixes above are the same: shorter showers and lower flow rates reduce the gas used.

I haven't included a saving for lowering the boiler's hot water temperature here, because the right setting depends on your boiler and there are safety points if you have a stored hot water tank. My post on [boiler flow temperature](/blog/boiler-flow-temperature-60-degrees-cut-gas-bill/) covers the heating side, and your boiler manual or installer should confirm what's safe for your system.

## The short version

- Shower time is the biggest lever. Four minutes instead of eight halves the cost of an electric shower, about £61 a year per daily shower at the price cap.
- A lower-flow shower head on a gas shower might save about £17 a year per daily shower on my assumptions. It depends on your own flow rate and habits.
- An immersion timer changes the price you pay, not the amount, so it only helps if you have a cheaper off-peak rate.
- A cylinder jacket cuts the heat that leaks away between showers.
