Short answer: **a slow cooker on low for eight hours uses about 1.3 kWh, which cost about 33p on a standard flat tariff in Yorkshire on 26 November 2026. Cooking with it twice a week comes to about £34.61 a year.**

That's less than half of what the same stew would cost in an electric oven for two hours, on the typical figures Cheap Watts uses. Eight hours sounds like a lot of electricity. It isn't, because a slow cooker draws very little power while it's on.

Here's the sum, how it compares with the oven, and what the time of day does to the price.

## The sum for one cook

Cheap Watts uses one typical figure for a slow cooker across the site: **1.3 kWh for eight hours on low, twice a week.**

That works out at an average of about 160 watts. [Electrical Safety First](https://electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/guidance/product-safety/slow-cookers) says "A slow cooker typically uses 150-300 watts of power but it's usually on for many hours", so the site's figure sits at the thrifty end of that range.

The cost of a cook is:

**kWh per cook × your price per kWh = the cost of the cook**

At the Flexible Octopus Direct Debit rate for Yorkshire on 26 November 2026, 25.60p per kWh, that's **about 33p a cook**. Twice a week is 104 cooks a year, or 135.2 kWh, which comes to **£34.61 a year**.

If yours is at the top of that 150 to 300 watt range, eight hours would use 2.4 kWh, or about 61p at the same rate. Still not much for a meal for the whole family, but worth knowing if your slow cooker is an older or bigger one.

## Slow cooker vs oven

Cheap Watts counts an electric oven as 1.5 kWh for an hour at 200°C. A casserole usually wants a couple of hours, so on that figure:

| How you cook it | Electricity used | Cost at 25.60p per kWh | Twice a week for a year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow cooker, 8 hours on low | 1.3 kWh | 33p | £34.61 |
| Electric oven, 2 hours | 3.0 kWh | 77p | £79.88 |

Two caveats, so you can judge the table fairly:

- The oven figure is for 200°C. A casserole cooked lower would use a bit less, so treat the oven row as a rough guide rather than a precise number.
- The same Electrical Safety First page puts ovens at 2,000 to 3,000 watts, against 150 to 300 for a slow cooker. An oven has to heat a big empty box. A slow cooker heats the pot and not much else.

So the slow cooker wins on running cost, but not by enough to justify buying one just to save money if you rarely cook stews. Where it really earns its place is cheap cuts of meat, big batches and food that's ready when you walk in. I went through the oven, air fryer and microwave for everyday meals in [air fryer vs oven vs microwave](/blog/air-fryer-vs-oven-vs-microwave-cheapest-per-meal/), if you're weighing up other kitchen kit.

## What the time of day does to the price

On a flat tariff, it doesn't matter when you switch it on. Every hour costs the same.

On a time-of-use tariff it does, and a slow cooker is unusual because it runs for so long that it nearly always crosses from one price into another. These are the Direct Debit unit rates for Yorkshire from the [Octopus price data](https://api.octopus.energy/v1/products/) on 26 November 2026, for the same 1.3 kWh over eight hours.

| Tariff and start time | Prices it runs through | One cook |
|---|---|---|
| Flexible Octopus (flat), any time | 25.60p all day | 33p |
| Cosy Octopus, 8am to 4pm | 27.72p, then 13.59p from 1pm | 29p |
| Cosy Octopus, 10am to 6pm | 27.72p, 13.59p, then 41.58p from 4pm | 34p |
| Octopus Go, daytime | 33.06p all day | 43p |
| Octopus Go, 12.30am to 8.30am | 9.40p until 5.30am, then 33.06p | 24p |

To keep the sums simple, I've spread the 1.3 kWh evenly across the eight hours. A real slow cooker won't draw power perfectly evenly, so treat these as estimates. On Cosy Octopus the cheap hours that day were 4am to 7am, 1pm to 4pm and 10pm to midnight, and 4pm to 7pm was the dear peak.

A few things stand out:

- **On Cosy, the start time matters a little.** Timing it to finish by 4pm keeps it out of the peak and catches three cheap hours. That's about 4p a cook, or roughly £4 a year at two a week. Nice, but not life changing.
- **On Octopus Go, the daytime rate is dearer than a flat tariff.** A slow cooker run during the day costs more on Go than on Flexible Octopus. Go is built around cheap night hours, and Octopus describes it as "the smart EV tariff", so check whether it suits your whole household before planning meals around it.
- **The cheapest slot is overnight.** That raises a safety question.

## Leaving it on: what the safety advice says

Most people buy a slow cooker so they can leave it while they're at work. [Electrical Safety First](https://electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/guidance/product-safety/slow-cookers) says "if you want to leave the house while it is running, set it on low. Only use the high heat option when you are in the house."

It also says to:

- set it on a heat-proof surface, at least six inches away from the wall
- keep the power cable away from the edges of the cooker
- not overfill it: typically no more than three-quarters full, and less for foods that expand, such as rice
- check the plug, socket and cable regularly for burn or scorch marks
- unplug it when you're not using it
- register it with the manufacturer, so you hear about any safety recall

Running it overnight while you sleep is a different question, and that page doesn't cover it. Fire services generally prefer appliances to run while someone's awake. [Devon and Somerset Fire and Rescue](https://www.dsfire.gov.uk/news/safety/why-you-shouldnt-use-washing-machines-and-tumble-dryers-night) says "The safest time to use electricals is when you are awake, and definitely in the house." The overnight saving on Go is about 19p a cook against the daytime rate. Weigh that against your own circumstances, and make sure your smoke alarms work.

## Low or high?

On high, a slow cooker uses more power for fewer hours. I don't have a reliable typical figure for high, so I'm not going to guess at a cost. If you want to know, measure it: an energy monitoring plug shows exactly what a cook used, and you can swap that number into the sum above.

Remember the safety point too: high is for when you're at home.

## How to get the most from it

1. **Use low when you can.** It's the setting the safety advice says to use when you're out, and it's the one Cheap Watts' typical figure is based on.
2. **Cook a bigger batch.** One run cooks a full pot, so filling it (within the three-quarters limit) and freezing portions spreads the cost over more meals.
3. **Time it on a time-of-use tariff.** If your tariff has a cheap afternoon window, aim to finish within it and before the teatime peak.
4. **Measure your own.** A monitoring plug tells you whether yours sits at 150 watts or 300, which is the difference between about 33p and 61p a cook.
