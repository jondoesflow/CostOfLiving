Short answer first: **if you're on a standard variable tariff you can switch at any time, free. On a fixed tariff you can leave without an exit fee once you have 49 days or less left. Your new supplier does the work, you get 14 days to change your mind, and the switch itself should happen within 5 working days. If it doesn't, the new supplier owes you £40.**

That's the headline. The details below come from [Citizens Advice's switching guide](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/get-a-better-energy-deal/switching-energy-supplier/) (written for England) and [Ofgem's page on switching supplier](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/your-energy-supply/how-manage-your-energy-supply/switch-your-home-energy-supplier), both read on 20 November 2026, plus exit fees from the Octopus, EDF and E.ON Next public APIs on the same day.

## Can you switch?

Start with your latest bill or your online account and find the name of your tariff. That decides most of it.

- **Standard variable tariff.** You can switch whenever you like.
- **Fixed tariff with 49 days or less to run.** You can switch without paying an exit fee.
- **Fixed tariff with 50 days or more to run.** You might have to pay an exit fee. Ask your supplier what it would be.

There are a few cases where you can't switch, or not easily:

- **You owe money.** Ofgem says you can still switch if you've been in debt to your supplier for less than 28 days (it goes on your final bill), or if you have a prepayment meter and owe up to £500. If you've been in debt for more than 28 days, you need to repay it first.
- **Your landlord pays the energy bills.** Then it's your landlord's switch to make, not yours. If you pay the bills yourself, you can switch, but Ofgem suggests checking your tenancy agreement first for a list of approved suppliers or a clause saying you must switch back when you leave.
- **You're on a heat network.** You can't switch, because one supplier runs the heating for the whole building.

When a fixed tariff ends and you do nothing, you'll usually be moved onto your supplier's standard variable tariff. That's often why people end up paying more than they need to: nobody chose it, it just happened.

## What exit fees look like right now

Exit fees are set per fuel, so a dual fuel customer leaving early pays one for electricity and one for gas. This is what three suppliers' current fixed tariffs charge, with Octopus's variable tariff for comparison, from their public APIs on 20 November 2026:

| Tariff | Exit fee per fuel |
| --- | --- |
| Octopus 12M Fixed | £50 |
| Octopus 18M Fixed | £75 |
| EDF Simply Fixed Nov27 | £50 |
| E.ON Next Fixed 12m | £50 |
| Flexible Octopus (variable) | none |

So leaving a 12 month fix early with both fuels would cost £100 at any of those three suppliers. Other suppliers and older tariffs can differ, and your fee is the one in your own contract, so check that rather than assume.

## Is it worth paying the exit fee?

Citizens Advice makes a point I agree with: you might still save more by switching, even after the fee. It's simple arithmetic.

Take the kWh you'd use in the time left on your fix, multiply by the difference in unit rates, and add the difference in standing charges over those days. Compare that with the exit fee.

Here's a worked example using my own assumptions, not your figures. A home uses 2,700 kWh of electricity a year and finds a tariff 2p per kWh cheaper with the same standing charge. That saves 2,700 × 2p = £54 a year. Against a £50 electricity exit fee, that's £4 better off over a full year, and worse off if you only had a few months left on the fix. On those numbers I'd sit tight and switch once I was inside the 49 days.

The bigger the gap or the higher your usage, the more an early switch makes sense. Just do the sum before you assume.

Don't assume a fix is the cheaper option either. On 20 November 2026, Octopus's 12 month fix for London was 28.98p per kWh for electricity against 26.60p on its Flexible tariff, with the same 41.39p daily standing charge. You pay that premium for certainty: the price can't go up during the fix, but it can't come down either. Citizens Advice says the same. Nobody knows which way prices will move, so choose the tariff you can budget for.

## How to switch, step by step

1. **Compare tariffs.** Your current supplier's other tariffs are worth a look too: Ofgem says the best option may be with your current supplier. If you use a comparison site, try more than one, as Citizens Advice notes they don't all show the same tariffs.
2. **Check your meter will work.** If you have a smart meter, ask whether it will keep working in smart mode after the switch. If you have a prepayment meter, you'll need a prepayment tariff, and not every supplier offers one. If you have an Economy 7 or other time-of-use meter, ask the new supplier whether their tariff works with it.
3. **Have your bill handy.** Ofgem lists what the new supplier will need: your postcode, your current supplier and tariff, what you pay per kWh (your unit rate) and your yearly usage.
4. **Sign up with the new supplier.** Online or by phone. You don't need to tell your old supplier you're leaving, because the new one does that.
5. **Take a meter reading on switch day** and give it to the new supplier, so the new supplier doesn't charge you for energy you used before the switch. A photo of the meter is the best receipt you'll ever get.

## How long does it take?

You choose when the switch happens:

- **As soon as possible.** This should take up to 5 working days. If you agree the contract before 5pm on a working day, the count starts that day. Otherwise it starts the next working day.
- **After the cooling-off period.** The switch then happens within 5 working days of the 14 days ending, which Citizens Advice says could be up to 21 days after you sign up.

If you want a specific date, such as the day your fix ends, ask the new supplier whether they can do that.

Working days mean Monday to Friday, not bank holidays. If you haven't been switched after 5 working days, check the new supplier has everything they need. If they have, Ofgem says they must pay you £40 automatically, into your energy account or your bank account.

## Changing your mind

You get a 14 day cooling-off period, starting the day after you agree the contract, during which you can cancel without a fee. Tell the new supplier.

If the switch hasn't happened yet, they stop it and you stay where you were. If it has already happened, the new supplier should explain your options, and you'll have 15 working days to pick one: a new contract with them, a contract with your old supplier on similar terms to what you had, or a different supplier. If you do nothing, you'll be put on the new supplier's standard variable tariff.

## Your old supplier's final bill

Your old supplier should send a final bill within 6 weeks. If you're in credit, they must refund it within 10 working days of sending that bill. If either is late, Citizens Advice says they might owe you compensation.

If you were switched without asking, contact your old supplier. Ofgem says you could get £40, and they have 21 working days to switch you back.

And if your supplier goes out of business, you don't need to do anything. Ofgem moves you to a new supplier and your supply carries on. Once that's done, you can switch as normal.

## What to do today

Find your tariff name and end date on your bill. If you're on a fix, put a reminder in your calendar for 49 days before it ends, because that's when you can move for free and before you drift onto the variable rate.

Then work out what you'd really pay elsewhere.
